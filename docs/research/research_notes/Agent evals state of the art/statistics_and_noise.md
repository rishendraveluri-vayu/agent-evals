# Statistics and noise: deciding whether config A beats config B on noisy agent evals (state as of 3 Nov 2026)

Scope: a platform running ~20 tasks under 2 configs with N repeated trials per task, binary pass/fail per trial. Needs: CIs, a paired test, per-task deltas, a "just noise" verdict, a defensible N from a pilot.

Notation used throughout: n = number of tasks (20 here), K or T = trials per task per config, p = pass rate, d_i = per-task difference in pass rate (A minus B), ρ or ICC = intraclass correlation (share of variance that is between-task), DEFF = design effect.

All numbers marked "my calculation" are derived by me from the cited formulas and are not in any source.

---

## Key Question 1: What do the authoritative treatments say (Miller 2024, Madaan et al. 2024, Biderman et al. 2024) and what have 2025–2026 follow-ups added?

### Takeaway
Miller (Anthropic, Nov 2024) is the canonical recipe: CLT standard errors, clustered SEs when questions are grouped, resampling to cut within-question variance, question-level paired differences, and power analysis; its formulas directly fit a tasks-x-trials design. The 2025–2026 literature confirms the core problem empirically for agents (single-run pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified moves 2.2–6.0 pp between runs; naive SEs are 40–60% too small once judge/prompt/temperature variance is counted) and adds ICC-based variance decomposition, pass^k, cluster bootstraps, and tooling (Inspect `stderr(cluster=)`, `evalci`).

### Cited Findings

**Miller, "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Anthropic blog, Nov 2024; arXiv:2411.00640)**
- Five recommendations: "Computing standard errors of the mean using the Central Limit Theorem"; "When questions are drawn in related groups, computing clustered standard errors"; "Reducing variance by resampling answers and by analyzing next-token probabilities"; inference on "question-level paired differences" when comparing two models; "Using power analysis to determine whether an eval (or a random subsample) is capable of testing a hypothesis of interest" — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- SE_CLT = sqrt(Var(s)/n); for binary scores SE_Bernoulli = sqrt(p̂(1−p̂)/n); 95% CI = mean ± 1.96·SE. The CLT "is applicable to any evals having scores with finite variance and a large number of questions"; bootstrapping is called unnecessary in that regime — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Clustered SE: SE_clustered = ( SE_CLT² + (1/n²) Σ_c Σ_i Σ_{j≠i} (s_{i,c} − s̄)(s_{j,c} − s̄) )^{1/2}. In Table 4, clustered errors were up to about 3x naive on DROP; the blog says clustered SEs on popular evals were "over three times as large as naive standard errors" — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640); [Anthropic blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)
- Law of total variance: Var(μ̂) = (Var(x) + E[σ_i²]) / n, where Var(x) is the variance of per-question true means (a property of the super-population, "immutable") and E[σ_i²] is the mean within-question sampling variance. With K resamples per question, Var(s_i) = σ_i²/K, so Var(μ̂) = (Var(x) + E[σ_i²]/K)/n. "Increasing n reduces both terms. Increasing K reduces only the conditional-variance term." — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Worked example (uniform difficulty, binary): Var(x) = 1/12, E[σ²] = 1/6; Var(μ̂ | K) = Var(μ̂ | K=1) × (1 + 2/K)/3. K=2 cuts variance by 1/3, K=4 by 1/2, K=6 by 5/9; the ceiling from resampling is a 2/3 reduction — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- "Don't touch the thermostat!": lowering temperature can shift variance into conditional means or add bias; in the example Var rose from 1/12 to 1/4 — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Paired comparison: SE_{A−B,paired} = sqrt(Var(s_{A−B})/n) = sqrt(SE_A² + SE_B² − 2·SE_A·SE_B·Corr(s_A, s_B)); Var(μ̂_paired) = Var(μ̂_unpaired) − 2Cov(x_A, x_B)/n. Observed correlations between frontier models on popular evals were "between 0.3 and 0.7"; at correlation 0.5 the example estimator variance drops from 1/6 to 1/9 — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640); [Anthropic blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)
- Clustered paired SE: SE = (1/n) ( Σ_c Σ_i Σ_j (s_{A−B,i,c} − s̄_{A−B})(s_{A−B,j,c} − s̄_{A−B}) )^{1/2} — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Power: n = (z_{α/2} + z_β)² (ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B) / δ², with ω² = Var(x_A) + Var(x_B) − 2Cov(x_A, x_B). MDE for fixed n: δ = (z_{α/2}+z_β)·sqrt((ω² + σ_A²/K_A + σ_B²/K_B)/n). Worked: σ²=0, ω²=1/9, δ=0.03, α=0.05, β=0.20 → n ≈ 969 (hence "at least 1,000 questions" for new evals); with σ_A²=σ_B²=1/6, ω²=1/9, n=198, raising K from 1 to 10 cuts MDE "from 13.2% to 7.5%". Appendix C gives clustered versions of ω², σ_A², σ_B² — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Recommendation when comparing models: report mean differences, SEs, CIs and correlations — [Anthropic blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)

**Madaan et al., "Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks" (arXiv:2406.10229, Jun 2024)**
- 10 seeds of a Llama-2-7B-architecture model, 21 checkpoints each. Seed std (pp) / 95% CI width: MMLU 0.57 / 0.72; GSM8k 0.41 / 0.87; HellaSwag 0.21 / 0.93; ARC-C 0.80 / 2.74; HumanEval 1.11 / 3.98; COPA 2.15 / 8.30; MATH 0.23 / 0.28 — [arXiv 2406.10229](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10229v1)
- Continuous metrics have higher SNR than discrete on every benchmark (MMLU discrete 52.45 vs continuous 347.57; GSM8k 7.88 vs 15.24) — [arXiv 2406.10229](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10229v1)
- Item analysis gave only "modest improvements"; IRT-selected 100-item subsets raised seed variance (ARC-C 0.80 → 1.80/1.86; HellaSwag 0.21 → 2.06/2.42), Kendall τ fell to 0.76 with ~12% of pairwise comparisons flipping. Recommendation: do not use item analysis/IRT subsets to reduce variance for model comparisons — [arXiv 2406.10229](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10229v1)
- Abstract: practitioners should "carefully factor in variance when comparing models" — [arXiv abs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10229)

**Biderman et al., "Lessons from the Trenches on Reproducible Evaluation of Language Models" (arXiv:2405.14782, 2024)**
- lm-eval-harness: "We report bootstrapped standard error metrics by default"; bootstrap intervals answer a different question than rerunning a stochastic model (they treat questions as "samples from some distribution of interesting questions in the world") — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)
- A single-run point estimate vs a 95% CI across 10 runs of GPQA "lead to very different conclusions about the relative performance of models" — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)
- Small benchmarks: gains on HumanEval (164 examples) "might be washed out simply via sampling again at the same temperature" — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)
- Prompt sensitivity: "performance varies dramatically with the prompt (in some cases, >20%)"; GPT-NeoX on ARC-Easy: cloze 72.4±1.80% vs MMLU-style 26.5±1.78% — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)
- Appendix D checklist: "Perform statistical significance testing"; most LM work does not (citing Marie et al. 2021); results "run over more than one random seed can dramatically boost the validity and utility of results"; "Always provide model outputs" (useful for significance testing) — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)

### Inferences
- Miller's decomposition maps one-to-one onto this platform: "questions" = tasks (n=20), "resamples" = trials (K=N). Everything downstream (clustered SE, paired differences, power) should be computed on per-task means, not on raw trials.
- The 2025–2026 agent-eval papers all land on the same floor: single-run or small-K deltas under ~3 pp on large benchmarks are indistinguishable from noise (Bjarnason, Segato). On a 20-task suite the floor is far higher (see Q5).
- Madaan's and Heineman's "continuous beats discrete" result suggests that if the platform can record a continuous score (rubric score, partial credit, cost, steps) alongside pass/fail, comparisons will have higher SNR than pass rate alone.

## Key Question 2: Unit of analysis — trials nested in tasks. How should the clustered structure be handled, and what goes wrong when 400 trials are treated as 400 independent samples?

### Takeaway
Trials within a task share the task's difficulty, so they are not independent; the correct variance of the pass rate is σ²_b/n + σ²_w/(nT) (between-task variance only shrinks with more tasks), and the naive sqrt(p(1−p)/(nT)) understates the SE by a factor of roughly sqrt(1 + (T−1)·ICC). With ICC values of 0.3–0.77 measured on agent benchmarks and T=20, that is a 3–4x understatement, which matches the >45% false-positive rates seen for naive tests on hierarchical data. Fixes in increasing complexity: cluster-robust (Miller) SE on per-task means; cluster bootstrap over tasks; two-stage hierarchical bootstrap (tasks, then trials); GLMM with task random intercepts.

### Cited Findings
- Variance with T trials per task: Var(μ̂) = σ²_b/n + σ²_w/(nT); with fixed budget B = nT this is σ²_b/n + σ²_w/B, so "for fixed B, maximizing n minimizes variance until the item pool is exhausted". Example: B=400, σ²_b/σ²_w ≈ 5: n=100, T=4 gives 68% lower SE than n=10, T=40 — [arXiv 2512.06710](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06710v1)
- Miller: "Increasing n reduces both terms. Increasing K reduces only the conditional-variance term"; naive CLT SEs are "inconsistent" when questions are grouped; clustered SE up to ~3x naive — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Design effect for clustered data: DE = 1 + (m̄ − 1)·ICC, where m̄ is mean cluster size; Kotawala applies it to MMLU-Pro subject clusters (median ICC 0.0010, heavy-tailed) and the unresolved count rises from 4/9 to 6/9 — [arXiv 2605.30315](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30315v1). The same clustering DEFF (1 + (m̄−1)ρ) and effective sample size n/DEFF appear in survey-methods references; Kish's formula "can be interpreted as a conservative value for the actual design effect" — [Statistics Canada, Gabler, Häder & Lahiri](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/12-001-x/1999001/article/4718-eng.pdf); [MetricGate design-effect note](https://metricgate.com/docs/design-effect-cluster/)
- Saravanan, Berman & Sober (hierarchical bootstrap, 2020): with nested data, "traditional statistical tests can result in a false positive rate of over 45%, even if the Type-I error rate is set at 5%"; simulation: traditional test ~46% at 10 trials per unit, rising to almost 96% at 3000 trials; summarized (per-unit means) and hierarchical bootstrap both ≈5%. Procedure: resample top-level units with replacement, then within each selected unit resample its trials with replacement (same count), compute the statistic; 10^4 bootstrap iterations; significance via bootstrap probability (p_boot ≥ 0.975 or ≤ 0.025 at α=0.05). Caveat: on truly independent data the bootstrap had a conservative bias (0.66% false positives at α=0.05, SEM ~1.4x larger); with very few top-level units it "can weight trials within levels more heavily than one would expect"; with one unit it degenerates to the traditional test. Summarizing "greatly reduces the statistical power" relative to the bootstrap — [bioRxiv full text](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/819334v1.full); [arXiv 2007.07797](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07797)
- Hierarchical bootstrap "performs about as well as linear mixed models" in simulation and "seems to perform better than LMMs" on their two real datasets; "the way to resample may and should change based on the hypothesis being tested" — [NBDT version](https://nbdt.scholasticahq.com/article/13927-application-of-the-hierarchical-bootstrap-to-multi-level-data-in-neuroscience). Terminology: a cluster-only bootstrap resamples only clusters; resampling both levels is Field & Welsh's "two-stage bootstrap" — [lmeresampler, arXiv 2106.06568](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.06568.pdf)
- Bagdasarian, "Coordinated, Not Independent" (NeurIPS 2026): treating pseudo-replicated units as independent made naive CIs 1.8x to 21x narrower than cluster-corrected ones; naive 95% intervals covered truth only 13% of the time on controlled data; a cluster bootstrap raised coverage to 73% under extreme concentration; recommends using the cluster as the unit of analysis and running a design-effect calculation before scoring — [NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/178172)
- METR uses a hierarchical bootstrap "over task families, then tasks, then attempts" with per-observation weights (Family Size)^(−1/2) — [METR note](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-20-impact-of-modelling-assumptions-on-time-horizon-results/)
- Inspect: `stderr(cluster="category")`, `ci()` with clusters−1 df, `ci_wilson()` on an effective sample size from the clustered SE — [Inspect metrics](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/metrics.html)
- GLMM precedents in LLM evaluation: Bayesian binomial mixed model with item-level random intercepts as a sensitivity check (random-effects intervals narrower than cluster-robust GLM) — [arXiv 2605.06327](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06327); binomial GLMM with paper-level random intercept justified by ICC 0.25–0.29 as robustness check on a paired analysis — [arXiv 2605.20668](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.20668); mixed logistic models with random intercepts for item and item-by-LLM — [arXiv 2608.00685](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.00685); a psychology-methods paper recommends paired item-level difference analyses when the same items are reused across models — [arXiv 2507.04491](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.04491)
- Miller on paired clustered SE: formula given above (Q1) — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)

### Inferences
- Worked example (my calculation from the DEFF formula and the ICC paper's measured ICCs): 20 tasks × 20 trials = 400 trials per config at p=0.5. Naive SE = sqrt(0.25/400) = 0.025. With ICC = 0.5 (middle of the GAIA/FRAMES range), DEFF = 1 + 19×0.5 = 10.5, effective n ≈ 38, true SE ≈ 0.025 × sqrt(10.5) ≈ 0.081. With ICC = 0.7, DEFF = 14.3, SE ≈ 0.095. A naive 95% CI of ±4.9 pp is really ±16–19 pp. A z-test on the naive SE would call a 10 pp difference "significant" when it is ~1 SE of true noise.
- Equivalent statement via Miller: variance ∝ ρ + (1−ρ)/K (my rearrangement of (Var(x) + E[σ²]/K)/n with ρ = Var(x)/(Var(x)+E[σ²])). At ρ=0.5: K=1 → 1.00, K=4 → 0.625, K=8 → 0.5625, K=20 → 0.525, K→∞ → 0.50. Going from K=8 to K=20 trims the SE by ~3%; adding 5 tasks (n 20→25) trims variance by 20%.
- Practical recommendation stack for this platform (my synthesis of the sources): (1) primary: per-task pass rates p_{A,i}, p_{B,i}; d_i = p_{A,i} − p_{B,i}; mean and SE = SD(d)/sqrt(20); CI with t_{19} not z (Inspect's clusters−1 df convention; Miller's CLT regime assumes many questions). (2) Robustness: two-stage hierarchical bootstrap, 10^4 iterations: resample 20 tasks with replacement, then within each selected task resample N trials with replacement for A and for B, recompute mean d; report percentile or BCa interval. (3) Optional model-based check: logistic GLMM pass ~ config + (1 | task) [+ (config | task)]; ICC from the random-effects variance doubles as the pilot's ρ estimate. Saravanan's conservativeness warning applies if tasks turn out to be nearly independent of trial outcomes (ICC≈0), which is unlikely given measured agent ICCs.
- With 20 top-level units the hierarchical bootstrap is in Saravanan's "approaches 5% as unit count grows" regime (they show it is poorly behaved only at 1–few units), but it will be noticeably wide; that width is the honest answer.
- Report the ICC itself (as Mustahsan et al. recommend): it tells Rishi whether the configs' noise is mostly task difficulty (high ICC → more tasks needed) or trial-level flakiness (low ICC → more trials help).

- No source gives a hierarchical-bootstrap coverage study specifically for n≈20 clusters with binary outcomes; the n≈20 behaviour is extrapolated from Saravanan's 1–16 unit curves and general cluster-bootstrap theory.
- Could not retrieve the Rank Intervals paper's full model (Neuhof & Benjamini, arXiv:2606.08679); only that it is hierarchical with task-level pairwise rank CIs and conformal prediction intervals.

## Key Question 3: Paired tests — paired bootstrap, McNemar, permutation, paired t on per-task means. Which fits binary pass/fail with repeats, and what is the pairing unit when trials are not matched one-to-one?

### Takeaway
Pairing is by task, not by trial: trials are exchangeable within a task, so the natural paired observation is the per-task difference in pass rate d_i, which collapses the repeats and the clustering at once. On that vector of 20 d_i values, a paired t (Miller's recommendation), an exact sign-flip permutation test (2^20 ≈ 1M patterns, fully enumerable), and a cluster/paired bootstrap are all valid; McNemar is valid only on genuinely 1:1 paired binary outcomes and, applied to per-task majority outcomes, has at most 20 pairs and very low power.

### Cited Findings
- Miller: conduct inference on "question-level paired differences" rather than population summaries; paired SE = sqrt(Var(s_{A−B})/n); the gain is proportional to Corr(s_A, s_B), observed 0.3–0.7 between frontier models; clustered paired SE formula given — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Abbas et al.: paired analysis on per-question score differences d_i, SE = SD_d/√N, 95% CI d̄ ± 1.96·SE; "typically produces narrower confidence intervals and greater statistical precision" than pooling the two SEs — [arXiv 2507.06893](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06893v1)
- Mustahsan et al.: for two agents use McNemar on discordant pairs, χ² = (|n₀₁ − n₁₀| − 1)²/(n₀₁ + n₁₀), and paired bootstrap resampling of items for CIs on accuracy differences — [arXiv 2512.06710](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06710v1)
- McNemar: tests marginal homogeneity H₀: p_b = p_c on a 2×2 table of paired outcomes; χ² = (b − c)²/(b + c) with 1 df; Edwards continuity correction (|b − c| − 1)²/(b + c); exact version compares b to Binomial(b + c, 0.5) and doubles the tail; the traditional threshold for switching to exact is b + c < 25 (Wikipedia tags this "citation needed"); concordant cells a, d do not contribute, "so power can be low even when the total number of pairs is large"; simulations found exact and continuity-corrected versions "overly conservative", while the mid-p version "was almost as powerful as the asymptotic McNemar test" without exceeding nominal α (Fagerland et al. 2013) — [Wikipedia: McNemar's test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McNemar%27s_test); the b + c < 25 rule and `binom.test(b, b+c, 0.5)` also in [MedCalc](https://www.medcalc.org/en/book/mcnemar-test.php) and [r-statistics.co](https://r-statistics.co/McNemars-Test-in-R.html)
- Paired permutation (Fisher 1935 sign-flip): under H₀ each paired difference is symmetric about zero, so flipping signs leaves the null distribution unchanged; enumerating all 2^n sign patterns gives an exact reference distribution; assumes only independence and symmetry of the differences, weaker than the paired t's normality — [arXiv 2606.26781](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.26781). Full enumeration is practical to n ≈ 20–30 depending on implementation; Monte Carlo sign vectors beyond — [MetricGate paired permutation](https://metricgate.com/docs/permutation-test-paired/); [MetricGate exact paired](https://metricgate.com/docs/exact-permutation-paired/). Wilcoxon signed-rank is the ranked variant; zeros are dropped before ranking — [MIBE journal](https://books.publisso.de/en/journals/mibe/volume6/mibe000104)
- evalci's default claim format uses a paired permutation test ("paired permutation p=0.002, n=1,319") — [arXiv 2607.04429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04429)
- Kotawala: paired-difference variance for binary outcomes σ_D² = p_A q_A + p_B q_B − 2ρ·sqrt(p_A q_A p_B q_B); the unpaired-then-×(1−ρ) shortcut understates N* by ≈2x in close comparisons because "(1−ρ) is correctly applied to the single-arm variance p(1−p), but the paired-difference variance" carries Var(X_A)+Var(X_B); for a single pair q ≥ 1 ⇔ |T| ≥ ~2.80 at (0.05, 0.8) — [arXiv 2605.30315](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30315v1)
- Bjarnason et al. use an unpaired two-sample t on run-level pass@1 (not a paired analysis) — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150)
- Saravanan: summarizing to the top level (one number per unit) controls Type I but "greatly reduces the statistical power"; hierarchical bootstrap retains more power — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/819334v1.full)

### Inferences
- Pairing unit when trials are not matched 1:1 (my synthesis): trial j of A and trial j of B share nothing except the task, so pairing them is arbitrary; any trial-level McNemar would treat 20N correlated pairs as independent and inherit the Q2 understatement. The defensible pairing unit is the task: d_i = p̂_{A,i} − p̂_{B,i}. This is exactly Miller's "resample each question K times and use question-level averages as scores" followed by his paired-difference formula.
- Recommended primary test for 20 tasks (my synthesis): exact sign-flip permutation on the 20 d_i (2^20 = 1,048,576 patterns, enumerable in milliseconds), reporting the mean d̄, a 95% CI from the hierarchical bootstrap, and the paired t_{19} p-value as a cross-check. The permutation test needs no normality assumption, which matters because d_i with small N is discrete and often zero.
- McNemar on 20 tasks (my calculation): if each task is reduced to a majority pass/fail per config, there are at most 20 pairs; the exact binomial two-sided p for b discordant pairs all favouring A is 2·0.5^b, so p < 0.05 needs b ≥ 6 with c = 0 (p = 0.031); 5–0 gives 0.0625. Treat McNemar as a secondary descriptive ("A won k tasks outright, B won m"), not the headline.
- If N is unequal between configs on a task (e.g., infra failures removed, see Q9), d_i on per-task pass rates still works; the hierarchical bootstrap resamples whatever trials exist per task per config, so unequal N is handled naturally. A trial-level pairing cannot handle it at all.

### Gaps
- Fagerland et al. (2013) was seen only via Wikipedia's summary; the mid-p recommendation is secondhand.
- No source directly compares paired t vs sign-flip permutation vs cluster bootstrap on a ~20-task binary-outcome design; the ranking above is inferred from general properties.

## Key Question 4: Confidence intervals for a difference in pass rates — Wilson, Newcombe, bootstrap percentile vs BCa, Bayesian. Which are recommended at small n?

### Takeaway
For a single pass rate use Wilson (or Jeffreys) rather than Wald; for the difference of two independent pass rates use Newcombe's hybrid Wilson (method 10), which is accurate in small samples and near 0/1; for the paired, clustered difference that this platform actually needs, there is no closed form in the literature beyond Miller's clustered paired SE, so a t-interval on per-task differences plus a hierarchical bootstrap (BCa if skewed) is the defensible pair. Bayesian Beta-Binomial intervals are easy but prior-sensitive below n≈10 per cell.

### Cited Findings
- Brown, Cai & DasGupta (2001, Statistical Science 16:101–133): "the chaotic coverage properties of the Wald interval are far more persistent than is appreciated"; recommend Wilson or equal-tailed Jeffreys for small n and Agresti–Coull for larger n; for n > 40 Wilson, Jeffreys and Agresti–Coull are very similar — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1009213286); companion 2002 Annals paper: Agresti–Coull has somewhat conservative coverage and is always the longest — [Project Euclid, Annals](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-statistics/volume-30/issue-1/Confidence-Intervals-for-a-binomial-proportion-and-asymptotic-expansions/10.1214/aos/1015362189.short)
- Formulas: Wald p̂ ± z·sqrt(p̂(1−p̂)/n) ("narrows to zero width" at p̂ = 0 or 1). Wilson: (1/(1 + z²/n))·( p̂ + z²/(2n) ± (z/(2n))·sqrt(4n·p̂(1−p̂) + z²) ). Agresti–Coull: ñ = n + z², p̃ = (n_s + z²/2)/ñ, p̃ ± z·sqrt(p̃(1−p̃)/ñ). Clopper–Pearson: Beta quantiles B(α/2; x, n−x+1) to B(1−α/2; x+1, n−x), "never has less than the nominal coverage… but that means that it is usually conservative". Jeffreys: Beta(x + 1/2, n − x + 1/2) equal-tailed quantiles. Wilson variants "have been shown to be the most accurate and the most robust". Rule of three: with 0 successes the approximate 95% interval is (0, 3/n) — [Wikipedia: Binomial proportion CI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_proportion_confidence_interval)
- Newcombe (1998), Statistics in Medicine 17:873–890, compared eleven methods for the difference of two independent proportions; the hybrid Wilson score interval (his method 10) is the recommended one ("method 10 in his paper looks like a winner", implemented as `method="Nc"` in R's Epi package) — [Epi::ci.pd](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/Epi/html/ci.pd.html); NCSS: Newcombe recommended the modified Wilson score ("hybrid score") over Pearson/Yates chi-square — [NCSS Two Proportions](https://www.ncss.com/wp-content/themes/ncss/pdf/Procedures/NCSS/Two_Proportions.pdf); misty's default hybrid score interval has "been shown to be reliable in small samples (less than n = 30 in each sample)" — [misty::ci.prop.diff](https://rdocumentation.org/packages/misty/versions/0.7.0/topics/ci.prop.diff)
- Newcombe hybrid formula: with Wilson limits (l₁, u₁), (l₂, u₂): L = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) − sqrt((p̂₁ − l₁)² + (u₂ − p̂₂)²); U = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) + sqrt((u₁ − p̂₁)² + (p̂₂ − l₂)²); "Accurate near 0 or 1 and for small samples, where the Wald interval fails" — [MetricGate Newcombe](https://metricgate.com/docs/newcombe-difference-interval/) (vendor page; formula consistent with Epi/NCSS descriptions)
- Inspect: `ci()` offers `method="bootstrap"` (percentile) and recommends `ci_wilson()` for binary scores, with an effective sample size from the clustered SE when `cluster=` is set; `bootstrap_stderr` uses 1000 resamples by default — [Inspect metrics](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/metrics.html)
- BCa: DiCiccio & Efron (1996, Statistical Science 11:189–228) survey BCa, bootstrap-t, ABC and calibration, aiming "to improve upon the accuracy of the standard intervals"; second-order accuracy is a keyword — [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/euclid.ss/1032280214). Secondary: BCa's acceleration constant adjusts coverage "to be second order accurate" — [MedCalc BCa note](https://www.medcalc.org/en/manual/note-bcabootstrap.php); finite-population simulation: at n=100, BCa coverage ~91.9% vs percentile ~90.7% (statistic 1) and ~95.8% vs 93.7% (statistic 2), converging by n=1000 — [arXiv 1804.05274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.05274); health-economics Monte Carlo at n = 5, 10, 20: ~70% of coverage probabilities below 90%, all methods too wide for skewed distributions — [ISPOR 2025 poster](https://www.ispor.org/heor-resources/presentations-database/presentation-cti/ispor-europe-2025/poster-session-2-2/bootstrap-methods-for-confidence-interval-estimation-in-small-samples-implications-for-health-economics)
- Bayesian difference of proportions: independent Beta posteriors (e.g., Jeffreys Beta(0.5, 0.5) priors), simulate p₁ − p₂, take 2.5th/97.5th percentiles — [Zhang, Gutiérrez & Cepeda](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/EMIS/journals/RCE/ingles/V33/v33n1a05ZhangGutierrezCepeda.pdf); with n < 10 results are strongly prior-dependent, and equal-tailed vs HPD intervals differ near the boundary — [MetricGate Bayesian proportion test](https://metricgate.com/docs/bayesian-proportion-test/); Agresti–Caffo's frequentist interval "performance is surprisingly high even for small sample sizes" — [same Zhang et al.](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/EMIS/journals/RCE/ingles/V33/v33n1a05ZhangGutierrezCepeda.pdf)
- Miller: SE_Bernoulli applied to fractional scores is "conservative (too wide)"; the blog's CI is mean ± 1.96·SEM — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)

### Inferences
- Worked per-task example (my calculation): N=10 trials, 7/10 passes → Wilson 95% CI [0.397, 0.892] (width 0.50). Per-task delta 7/10 vs 3/10 (Δ = +40 pp) → Newcombe 95% CI [−0.03, +0.67], which includes zero. So at N=10 a per-task delta is individually uninformative unless it is near 10/10 vs 0/10; per-task CIs should be shown so this is visible, but headline inference must come from the pooled paired analysis.
- Which interval for the headline Δ (my synthesis): (a) t_{19} interval on the 20 per-task differences (Miller's paired SE, Inspect's clusters−1 df); (b) hierarchical-bootstrap interval, percentile at 10^4 resamples, BCa if the bootstrap distribution is visibly skewed (expected when the pass rates are near 0 or 1); (c) report both and flag disagreement. Newcombe is the right choice for per-task deltas (independent proportions within a task) but not for the pooled delta, since it ignores pairing and clustering.
- Bayesian option: a hierarchical Beta-Binomial or logistic GLMM gives a posterior on Δ that shrinks noisy per-task estimates toward the pool; with 20 tasks × N trials the prior matters below N≈10, so if used it should be reported alongside the frequentist intervals, not instead of them.

### Gaps
- Newcombe (1998) itself was not readable; the formula and the "method 10" recommendation come from R package documentation, NCSS and a vendor page that agree with each other.
- No primary source found that evaluates CI coverage for a paired, clustered difference of proportions with ~20 clusters; recommendations here combine Miller's formula with general bootstrap theory.

## Key Question 5: Choosing N — power analysis for a minimum detectable effect from pilot variance; when more trials per task stop helping; what pass^k adds

### Takeaway
Use Miller's MDE formula with pilot estimates of ω² (variance across tasks of the per-task A−B difference), σ_A², σ_B² (within-task Bernoulli variances) and n=20: δ = 2.80·sqrt((ω² + (σ_A² + σ_B²)/K)/20) at α=0.05, power 0.80. More trials only shrink the (σ_A² + σ_B²)/K term; once E[σ²]/K ≪ Var(x) (AISI's rule), the task count is the binding constraint, and with 20 tasks the MDE is likely to sit in the 6–25 pp range depending on how task-dependent the configs' difference is. pass^k adds a consistency metric (probability all k trials pass) that is what a user of an unattended agent experiences; it needs ≥k trials per task and its CI comes from the task bootstrap.

### Cited Findings
- Miller MDE and n formulas with worked numbers (n ≈ 969 for δ=3 pp when σ²=0, ω²=1/9; K 1→10 cuts MDE 13.2% → 7.5% at n=198) — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Miller: resampling ceiling in the uniform example is a 2/3 variance reduction; K=4 already gets 1/2 — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Abbas et al. (inspect_evals): pilot 100 samples × 10 epochs, compute E[σ_i²] and Var(x̄), choose K so E[σ_i²]/K ≪ Var(x̄); beyond that "additional resampling provides diminishing returns"; no numeric cutoff for "≪" is given — [arXiv 2507.06893](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06893v1)
- Mustahsan et al.: Var(μ̂) = σ²_b/n + σ²_w/(nT); at fixed budget maximize tasks; "ICC converges by n=8–16 trials for structured tasks and n≥32 for complex reasoning"; "even a modest replication (e.g., T≥5) substantially stabilizes estimates"; target SE(ICC) ≤ 0.03 (95% CI ≈ ±0.06); "Report both n and T alongside ICC values" — [arXiv 2512.06710](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06710v1)
- Bjarnason et al. (500-task SWE-bench Verified, run-level SD σ = 1.5%, unpaired two-sample t, power 0.80): "Detecting a 2% improvement at p<0.05 with 80% power requires approximately 9 runs per agent under test, while detecting a 1% improvement requires 36 runs"; Table 2: 2% → 9 (p<0.05), 14 (p<0.01), 20 (p<0.001); 1% → 36, 53, 77 — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150)
- Kotawala paired sample size: N* = (z_{1−α/2} + z_{1−β})²·σ_D²/(p_A − p_B)² with σ_D² = p_A q_A + p_B q_B − 2ρ·sqrt(p_A q_A p_B q_B); worked: (p_A, p_B, ρ) = (0.65, 0.60, 0.30) → N* = 1,028 paired items (the ×(1−ρ) shortcut gives 515); HellaSwag gemma-7b vs Llama-3-8B: N = 10,042, 0.8247 vs 0.8202, ρ̂ = 0.81, exact p = 0.054, N* = 20,255; resolution ratio q = N/N*, "resolvable at (α,1−β) if q≥1"; "q<1 does not assert equality of the two models" — [arXiv 2605.30315](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30315v1)
- Blackwell et al.: add repeats until the 95% prediction interval width < ~0.01; rarely more than 3 repeats needed on a 5,760-item benchmark; on the 100-item benchmark GPT-3.5T and Llama-3 7B never reached that width in 30 repeats — [arXiv 2410.03492](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03492v2)
- Heineman et al.: a benchmark's usefulness for decisions tracks SNR, not size; some reach higher SNR with ~10x fewer instances — [arXiv 2508.13144](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.13144v1)
- pass^k (τ-bench, Yao et al. 2024): "defined as the chance that all k i.i.d. task trials are successful, averaged across tasks"; unbiased estimator pass^k = E_task[ C(c, k)/C(n, k) ] with c successes in n trials; pass@k = 1 − E_task[ C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) ]; motivation: "real-world agent tasks requiring reliability and consistency like customer service"; gpt-4o pass^1 retail 61.2%, airline 35.2%; pass^8 retail "<25%"; main results used "at least 3 trials per task" — [arXiv 2406.12045](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1)
- Bjarnason et al. use the same estimators (pass@k = (1/N)Σ[1 − C(m−c_i,k)/C(m,k)], pass^k = (1/N)Σ C(c_i,k)/C(m,k)) and frame pass@k as an optimistic bound and pass^k as a pessimistic bound — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150)
- Inspect implements `pass_k_{k}` ("Probability that all k epoch attempts succeed") and `pass_at_{k}` as epoch reducers — [Inspect metrics](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/metrics.html)
- Epoch runs 16 repeats on GPQA Diamond/Mock AIME and 8 on MATH L5; METR typically 8 attempts per task; DeepSWE 4 runs — [Epoch FAQ](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/about); [METR note](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-20-impact-of-modelling-assumptions-on-time-horizon-results/); [Epoch DeepSWE](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/deepswe)

### Inferences
- MDE for this platform under Miller's illustrative variances (my calculation; z_{α/2}+z_β = 1.96+0.84 = 2.80): n=20, ω²=1/9, σ_A²=σ_B²=1/6. K=1: δ = 2.80·sqrt(0.444/20) = 0.42 (42 pp). K=10: 2.80·sqrt(0.144/20) = 0.24 (24 pp). K→∞: 2.80·sqrt(0.111/20) = 0.21 (21 pp). So with 20 tasks and configs whose per-task differences vary as much as Miller's example, nothing under ~20 pp is detectable no matter how many trials are run. If instead the two configs differ almost uniformly across tasks (ω² ≈ 0.01), K→∞ gives δ ≈ 2.80·sqrt(0.01/20) = 6.3 pp, and K=10 gives 2.80·sqrt((0.01+0.033)/20) = 13 pp. The pilot's job is therefore to estimate ω² (variance of d_i across tasks) and E[σ²] (mean of p_i(1−p_i) within task), and the MDE should be reported to Rishi as a function of K at n=20.
- Pilot design (my synthesis of Abbas et al. and Mustahsan et al.): run both configs on all 20 tasks with K_pilot = 5–8 trials (Mustahsan: T≥5 stabilizes accuracy; ICC converges by 8–16 for structured tasks). From it compute per-task p̂_{A,i}, p̂_{B,i}, d_i; ω̂² = Var(d_i) − mean(p̂_A(1−p̂_A)/K + p̂_B(1−p̂_B)/K) (removing the sampling part), Ê[σ²] = mean within-task Bernoulli variance, ρ̂ = ICC. Then solve for K such that Ê[σ²]/K ≤ ~0.1–0.2 × ω̂² (my operationalization of AISI's "≪"); report the MDE curve δ(K).
- Where trials stop helping: once (σ_A² + σ_B²)/K is a small fraction of ω², each doubling of K buys almost nothing (Q2 worked example: ρ=0.5, K 8→20 trims SE ~3%). The only way to lower the MDE further is more tasks (Miller, Mustahsan) or a continuous score with more signal (Madaan, Heineman).
- Bjarnason's "9 runs for 2 pp" is not transferable: it is for run-level aggregates over 500 tasks with σ=1.5%, unpaired. On 20 tasks the between-task term dominates and the comparable requirement is in tasks, not runs.
- pass^k: for this platform, report pass^k for k ∈ {1, 3, 5} alongside pass rate, computed with the unbiased C(c,k)/C(N,k) estimator (needs N ≥ k, ideally N ≥ 2k so the estimator is not degenerate); its CI should come from the same task-level hierarchical bootstrap, since it is a nonlinear function of per-task counts. Under within-task independence pass^k = p^k per task (follows from τ-bench's definition), so an 80%-per-trial task has pass^5 = 0.33; pass^k therefore magnifies differences in consistency that pass rate hides.

### Gaps
- No source gives a numeric threshold for AISI's "E[σ²]/K ≪ Var(x̄)"; the 0.1–0.2 factor above is my suggestion.
- No published power table for paired, clustered binary outcomes at n≈20 tasks; the MDE figures are from Miller's formula with illustrative variances.
- Did not confirm whether τ-bench ran ≥8 trials per task for pass^8 (text says "at least 3 trials").

## Key Question 6: Multiple comparisons across per-task deltas (Bonferroni, Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg) and how practitioners present per-task results without overclaiming

### Takeaway
Per-task deltas at N≈10–20 trials are individually underpowered (Q4 example: a +40 pp delta has a CI that includes zero), so the honest presentation is one pre-registered headline test on the pooled paired difference, with per-task deltas shown descriptively with Newcombe CIs; if per-task significance is reported, use Holm (FWER, valid under any dependence) for a short confirmatory list or BH (FDR) for exploratory flagging, and say which. Two 2026 re-analyses show that leaderboard-style claims routinely fail once corrected for the number of comparisons.

### Cited Findings
- Holm (1979) step-down: sort p-values P(1) ≤ … ≤ P(m); reject H(k) while P(k) ≤ α/(m+1−k); stop at the first failure; "This method ensures that the FWER is at most α, in the strong sense"; "uniformly more powerful than the Bonferroni correction"; adjusted p̃(i) = max_{j≤i} min{(m−j+1)·p(j), 1}; Hochberg's step-up is more powerful but needs independence or positive dependence, which Holm does not — [Wikipedia: Holm–Bonferroni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holm%E2%80%93Bonferroni_method)
- Benjamini–Hochberg (1995): FDR = expected proportion of rejected nulls that are false; sort p-values, find the largest k with P(k) ≤ (k/m)α, reject all H(i), i ≤ k; valid under independence and "various scenarios of dependence, but is not universally valid"; Benjamini–Yekutieli replaces α with α/c(m), c(m) = Σ_{i=1}^{m} 1/i under arbitrary dependence; if all nulls are true, controlling FDR at q also controls FWER at q; FDR procedures "provide less stringent control of Type I errors compared to family-wise error rate (FWER) controlling procedures" — [Wikipedia: False discovery rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_discovery_rate); original paper — [Benjamini & Hochberg 1995](https://www.dcscience.net/Benjamini-Hochberg-1995-FDR.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance: FWER (Holm) for confirmatory work or when "one wrong claim triggers an expensive or irreversible decision"; FDR (BH) for large exploratory screens; "a handful of pre-registered comparisons leans toward FWER, while hundreds or thousands lean toward FDR" — [MetricGate FDR vs FWER](https://metricgate.com/blogs/fdr-vs-fwer/) (vendor blog)
- evalci case study: of 8 adjacent rank gaps among nine models on MMLU, 3 are not significant after correcting for 36 pairwise comparisons — [arXiv 2607.04429](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04429)
- Kotawala: unresolved pairs "persist under multiplicity correction and anytime-valid sequential testing" — [arXiv 2605.30315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30315)
- Madaan et al.: IRT-selected subsets flipped ~12% of pairwise model comparisons — a warning about post-hoc task subsetting — [arXiv 2406.10229](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10229v1)
- Heineman et al. / Olmo 3: filtering noisy subtasks by SNR is done before use, to define the benchmark, and tasks too noisy to help development are excluded from the macro-average — [arXiv 2508.13144](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.13144v1); [Olmo 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.13961)
- Saravanan applied Bonferroni to their bootstrap comparisons — [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/819334v1.full)
- Inspect `grouped()` produces one metric per group plus an "all" metric, with `all="groups"` making "all" the mean of group values — a per-task/per-category reporting pattern — [Inspect metrics](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/metrics.html)

### Inferences
- Presentation recipe (my synthesis): (1) one headline: pooled paired Δ with CI and the pre-registered test's p; (2) a per-task table: n_A, n_B, p̂_A, p̂_B, Newcombe 95% CI for d_i, pass^k, infra-error counts; (3) sort by d_i but label the table "descriptive"; (4) if a per-task significance column is wanted, give Holm-adjusted p (m = 20) for the "safe to act on" list and BH q-values for "worth a look"; (5) state the number of tests up front. With m=20 and Holm, the smallest p needs to be < 0.0025 to be declared, which per-task Newcombe/Fisher tests at N=10 will almost never reach; that is a feature, not a bug.
- Task selection for the suite (pre-registration): decide before the experiment which tasks count; SNR-style filtering is legitimate only on pilot data, never after seeing A-vs-B results (consistent with the project rule to record any post-hoc analysis change as a deviation).

### Gaps
- No agent-eval paper found that specifically evaluates Holm vs BH on per-task deltas; the guidance is standard statistics applied by inference.

## Key Question 7: A/A tests, equivalence tests (TOST), and other ways to make "just noise" explicit

### Takeaway
"Not significant" is not "equivalent." Three complementary tools make the noise claim explicit: an A/A run (same config twice) that produces an empirical noise floor for the exact statistic used; a TOST equivalence test against a pre-declared smallest effect of interest (SESOI), equivalent to checking that the 90% CI lies inside ±SESOI; and Kotawala's resolution ratio q = N/N*, which states whether the design could have detected the gap in the first place. Together they yield a four-way verdict: better / equivalent / undetermined (underpowered) / different-but-trivial.

### Cited Findings
- TOST (Lakens; TOSTER vignette): "In the TOST procedure the null hypothesis is the presence of a true effect of ΔL or ΔU"; data are compared against ΔL and ΔU "in two one-sided tests"; if both are rejected, "we can follow a Neyman-Pearson approach to statistical inferences and reject effect sizes larger than the equivalence bounds"; TOSTER output reports a 90% "TOST confidence interval" next to the 95% NHST interval; bounds can be raw (e.g., "0.3 scale point on a 5-point scale") or standardized; outcome combinations include "statistically different from zero, and practically equivalent", "neither significantly different… nor statistically equivalent", and equivalent-and-not-significant — [TOSTER vignette](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/TOSTER/vignettes/IntroductionToTOSTER.html); tutorial: Lakens, Scheel & Isager (2018), AMPPS, DOI 10.1177/2515245918770963 — [Scinapse record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2788930055)
- A/A tests: both groups get the same experience, so any significant difference is a false positive; the purpose is to validate randomization, metric pipelines and that tests "produce false positives at the expected rate"; at α = 0.05 expect ~5 significant results per 100 A/A tests, and a single A/A test proves little, so run several (Statsig suggests ~5–10) and inspect the p-value distribution, which should be roughly uniform — [Statsig blog](https://statsig.com/blog/how-to-aa-test); [Statsig docs](https://docs.statsig.com/experiments/types/aa-test); [Spotify Confidence glossary](https://confidence.spotify.com/glossary/aa-testing); [Convert glossary](https://www.convert.com/glossary/a-a-testing/); [Wish / TDS](https://towardsdatascience.com/a-a-testing-establishes-trust-in-experimentation-platform-db30387d5bf9/). Kohavi, Tang & Xu's book has a dedicated chapter "The A/A test" (also indexed as "null test") within its statistics section — [Cambridge index](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811087/24265/index/9781108724265_index.pdf)
- Noise floor via reruns: Chen et al. measure "ever-flip" fractions and rerun paired SDs from repeated runs of the same configuration, then compare perturbation SDs against them (11–58x larger) — [arXiv 2608.22331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22331)
- Resolution ratio: q = N/N*(δ̂); "resolvable at (α,1−β) if q≥1"; "q<1 does not assert equality of the two models, nor does it overturn a fixed-N p-value"; for a single pair q = T²/(z_{1−α/2}+z_{1−β})², so q ≥ 1 ⇔ |T| ≥ ~2.80 at (0.05, 0.8) — [arXiv 2605.30315](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.30315v1)
- Segato: be skeptical of leaderboard gaps below 3 pp until infra is matched — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise). Bjarnason: 2–3 pp improvements "may reflect evaluation noise" — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07150)
- Miller: a hypothesis such as "Model A outperforms Model B by 3 percentage points" against a null of a tie, then compute the n needed — [Anthropic blog](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)
- Mustahsan et al.: report accuracy with ICC and within-query variance "as standard practice" so readers can judge stability — [arXiv 2512.06710](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.06710v1)

### Inferences
- A/A for this platform (my synthesis): run config A twice as two independent batches (A1, A2), interleaved in time with the A/B runs so provider drift and load are shared, and compute exactly the same statistic (paired per-task Δ, CI, permutation p) on A1 vs A2. The A/A Δ and its CI are the empirical noise floor; an A/B Δ smaller than, or with a CI overlapping, the A/A floor should be labelled "within noise floor". Repeating the A/A a few times (Statsig's 5–10) also checks that the pipeline's p-values are roughly uniform.
- TOST on this design: pre-declare SESOI (e.g., 5 or 10 pp in pass rate, chosen from what would change a deployment decision); compute the paired per-task difference's 90% CI (t_{19} or bootstrap); if it lies entirely within ±SESOI, report "equivalent at SESOI"; if the 95% CI excludes 0 and lies within ±SESOI, report "different but trivial". Note that with 20 tasks the 90% CI will often be wider than ±5 pp, so the honest outcome will frequently be "undetermined", which TOST makes explicit instead of letting a non-significant p imply equality.
- Verdict logic (my synthesis): (i) 95% CI excludes 0 and |Δ| ≥ SESOI → "A beats B" (or B beats A); (ii) 90% CI within ±SESOI → "equivalent"; (iii) CI excludes 0 but within ±SESOI → "different, not meaningful"; (iv) otherwise → "undetermined; design cannot resolve gaps smaller than MDE = δ(K)"; always print the A/A floor and q = N/N* next to the verdict.

### Gaps
- Kohavi's A/A chapter text was not retrievable; the expected-5%-false-positive rule is from experimentation-platform docs that cite the same logic.
- No source applies TOST specifically to paired, clustered pass-rate differences; the 90% CI equivalence is standard TOST theory applied by inference.

## Key Question 8: Sources of nondeterminism (temperature 0, batch invariance, provider-side changes, caching) and their effect on the independence of repeated trials

### Takeaway
Temperature 0 is not deterministic on hosted inference because kernels are not batch-invariant and server load (hence batch composition) varies; providers themselves describe seeds as best-effort. Run-to-run noise at temperature 0 is still ≥1.5 pp on agent benchmarks and can exceed default-temperature noise, provider behaviour drifts over weeks, and infrastructure resources shift results by up to 6 pp. For the platform this means trials are independent samples only if they are not cached, not served from a replayed response, and are spread across time so that shared load/version effects do not correlate them; Miller's advice is to leave temperature at its deployment value rather than chase determinism.

### Cited Findings
- He (Thinking Machines, 10 Sep 2025): LLM APIs are "not deterministic in practice" at temperature 0; the "concurrency + floating point" story is incomplete because the same matmul on the same data is bitwise reproducible; the real cause is lack of batch invariance — kernel numerics depend on batch size and "the load (and thus batch-size) nondeterministically varies!"; 1,000 temperature-0 completions of Qwen3-235B-A22B gave 80 unique completions (most common appeared 78 times), identical for the first 102 tokens; with batch-invariant RMSNorm, matmul and attention kernels all 1,000 were identical; cost: vLLM 26 s default vs 55 s unoptimized deterministic vs 42 s with an improved attention kernel; non-identical numerics between sampler and trainer "implicitly turns our on-policy RL into off-policy RL" — [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)
- Bjarnason et al.: SD "exceeding 1.5 percentage points even at temperature 0"; "the variance never decreases and sometimes increases with temperature zero" (e.g., Qwen3-32B/nano-agent 0.7% at T=0.6 vs 1.2% at T=0) — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150)
- Blackwell et al.: nondeterminism persists "even when setting temperature to zero with a fixed random seed"; only locally-run Llama-3 7B was deterministic; GPT-3.5T via Azure (0.833) vs OpenAI (0.840) differed significantly (t=2.51, p=0.013); temperature 0 + seed shifted GPT-3.5T's mean from 0.55 to 0.59 — [arXiv 2410.03492](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.03492v2)
- Chen et al. (Noise Floor Audit): on BFCL, temperature-0 reruns on some endpoints were nearly deterministic (ever-flip 0.7–2.7%), yet semantics-preserving prompt perturbations produced paired SDs 11–58x larger than rerun SDs — [arXiv 2608.22331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22331)
- OpenAI: the `seed` parameter makes the system "try to sample deterministically"; "Determinism is not guaranteed"; monitor `system_fingerprint`, which identifies the backend weights/infrastructure/configuration; if seed, parameters and fingerprint match, outputs "will mostly be identical" with "a small chance responses differ due to the inherent non-determinism of the models" — [OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/reproducible_outputs_with_the_seed_parameter)
- Anthropic Messages API: third-party reproductions of the API reference state that even at temperature 0.0 results "will not be fully deterministic" (the reference page itself was too large to extract the line directly) — [camel-ai Anthropic config reference, citing docs.anthropic.com](https://docs.camel-ai.org/reference/camel.configs.anthropic_config)
- Miller: "Don't touch the thermostat!" — lowering temperature can move variance into the conditional means or add bias (example variance 1/12 → 1/4) — [arXiv 2411.00640](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)
- Provider drift: Chen, Zaharia & Zou tracked GPT-3.5/GPT-4 between March and June 2023; "the behavior of the 'same' LLM service can change substantially in a relatively short amount of time"; GPT-4 prime-identification accuracy 97.6% (March) → 2.4% (June) in v1 of the paper (later versions report different figures) — [arXiv 2307.09009](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09009v1); [HDSR](https://hdsr.mitpress.mit.edu/pub/y95zitmz/release/1)
- Infrastructure: Segato found a 6 pp gap on Terminal-Bench 2.0 between least- and most-resourced setups; infra error rate 5.8% → 0.5%; time of day "observed anecdotally to affect pass rates"; recommends treating resources as a controlled variable and running "at multiple times and on multiple days" — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise)
- Messing: ignoring judge, temperature and prompt variation leaves naive SEs 40–60% too small; recommends averaging over them rather than fixing them — [arXiv 2604.11581](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.11581)
- Biderman et al.: prompt formatting alone can move scores >20 pp — [arXiv 2405.14782](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.14782v3)
- LiteLLM caching: "stores and reuses LLM responses"; exact-match caches "key on a hash of the whole request, so any change to the conversation is a miss"; "When you make the same request twice, the cached response is returned instead of calling the LLM API again"; when enabled with no `supported_call_types`, caching covers `/chat/completions`, `/completions`, `/v1/messages` and others; cached responses carry an `x-litellm-cache-key` header — [LiteLLM docs](https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/proxy/caching)
- Inspect's `retry_on_error` warns that retrying errored samples "can cause distribution shift", so retried and non-retried results should be compared — [Inspect handling errors](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/handling-errors.html.md)

### Inferences
- Independence of repeated trials (my synthesis): a trial is an independent draw only if (a) it is a fresh API call (any response cache would turn N trials into N copies of one draw, collapsing within-task variance to zero and making SEs meaningless — hence the project's no-cache rule); (b) nothing deterministic ties trials together (same seed + same fingerprint would make them near-copies, which is the opposite of what repeated trials are for); (c) shared-state effects are either randomized or shared equally between A and B. Since batch composition and load vary by the second, consecutive trials at temperature 0 are not identical but are plausibly correlated through load; interleaving A and B trials and spreading them over time turns that correlation into something the paired design absorbs.
- Record per trial: provider, model version string, `system_fingerprint` (OpenAI) or equivalent, timestamp, temperature, token counts, and the `x-litellm-cache-key` header's absence; group-by fingerprint lets a reviewer check for a mid-experiment backend change (the Chen/Zaharia/Zou failure mode).
- Do not set temperature 0 to "reduce noise": Miller argues it changes the estimand and Bjarnason shows it does not reduce run-level variance for agents. Evaluate at the temperature the config will ship with.

### Gaps
- Could not quote Anthropic's own temperature wording from platform.claude.com (page too large to parse); the statement is corroborated only via third-party reproductions.
- No direct measurement found of how strongly temporally adjacent API calls are correlated (as opposed to merely non-identical); the "spread over time" advice is precautionary.
- LiteLLM's per-request `no-cache` control and default TTL were not on the fetched page.

## Key Question 9: How should infra failures and timeouts be treated in the analysis (exclude, count as fail, report separately)?

### Takeaway
The emerging convention (Epoch/DeepSWE, Group of Skills, Inspect's defaults) is a three-way outcome: agent-attributable limits (timeouts, context exhaustion, step/token caps) are scored as failures because the agent had its chance; provider/infrastructure errors (API outages, sandbox crashes, OOM during setup) are excluded from the pass-rate denominator and reported separately with their rate, not silently dropped and not resampled; and because infra error rates are themselves config- and resource-dependent (5.8% vs 0.5% in Anthropic's study), the error rate must be shown per config alongside the pass rate, with a sensitivity analysis that counts infra errors as failures.

### Cited Findings
- DeepSWE (Jul 2026): "We distinguish failures that reflect model capability from artifacts of the evaluation infrastructure. Exhausting the model's context window before submitting a patch, and exceeding the agent's wall-clock budget (an agent timeout), are scored as genuine failures"; excluded rollouts are not resampled; exclusions run 0% to 5.3% across configurations — [arXiv 2607.07946](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07946); Epoch's page: "Context-window failures and agent timeouts count as failures" while "provider and infrastructure errors are excluded from scoring" — [Epoch DeepSWE](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/deepswe)
- Group of Skills (2026): infrastructure failures such as API outages or environment crashes are excluded and not imputed; agent timeouts stay in as task failures when the evaluator still returns a result — [arXiv 2605.06978](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06978)
- Inspect: sample limits (time_limit, working_limit, message_limit, token_limit, turn_limit, cost_limit) are not errors — "samples that encounter limits are still scored, albeit nearly always as 'incorrect'"; `working_limit` counts only active work, excluding rate-limit retries and waits on shared resources; "Always set a time_limit alongside a working_limit to bound clock time" — [Inspect setting limits](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/setting-limits.html.md). Sample errors: default `fail_on_error=True` fails the eval immediately; a fraction (0.1 = 10% of samples) or count (5) can be tolerated; `retry_on_error` retries before counting; `score_on_error` scores errored samples from the state reached; otherwise failed samples are "not scored" and do not contribute to metrics; retrying "can cause distribution shift"; tolerate errors in development but "ensure there are never errors when running in production" — [Inspect handling errors](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/handling-errors.html.md)
- Segato (Anthropic): infra error rate 5.8% at 1x resources, 2.1% at 3x (p<0.001), 0.5% uncapped; from 1x to 3x success rate was within noise (p=0.40) because "Most tasks that crashed at 1x would have failed anyway"; from 3x to uncapped, infra errors fell 1.6 points and success rose almost 4 points because agents attempted resource-heavy approaches; the capability/infrastructure boundary is "blurry" — [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/infrastructure-noise)
- Chen et al. (Noise Floor Audit): malformed-output failures made up 30%, 7% and <1% of task failures across endpoints; "marginal accuracy hides failure-mode shifts" — [arXiv 2608.22331](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22331)
- Abbas et al.: for agentic evaluations contributors must check logs to confirm agents "aren't failing on obvious fixable issues", and to prevent tool failures from "artificially degrad[ing] model performance" — [arXiv 2507.06893](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06893v1)
- Epoch's hub: a response that does not follow the required answer format gets "no points for that question" (format failures are failures) — [Epoch FAQ](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/about)
- Bjarnason et al. do not state how crashed trajectories were handled; they only report run limits (500 tool calls for nano-agent, 100 for r2e-gym) — [arXiv 2602.07150](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.07150)

### Inferences
- Label taxonomy for the platform (my synthesis): per trial, `outcome ∈ {pass, fail, infra_error}` plus `fail_reason ∈ {wrong_answer, timeout, step_limit, token_limit, malformed_output, …}` and `infra_reason ∈ {provider_5xx, rate_limit_exhausted, sandbox_crash, oom_setup, …}`. Primary pass rate = pass / (pass + fail), computed per task per config; infra_error rate reported per config with its own CI; the two numbers travel together.
- Why not count infra errors as fails by default: Segato's data show infra error rate depends on resources and config, so counting them as fails would let an infra artefact masquerade as a capability difference; why not drop them silently: they can be 5–6% of trials and may be correlated with the harder tasks ("would have failed anyway"), so their removal biases the pass rate upward. The sensitivity analysis "infra errors counted as fails" gives a lower bound on each config's pass rate; if the verdict flips between the two analyses, the verdict is "undetermined".
- Timeouts: follow DeepSWE/Inspect and score as fail, but set the time limit on working time, not wall clock, so provider rate-limiting does not create spurious failures, and bound wall clock separately (Inspect's two-limit advice).
- Retries: if a trial is retried after a provider error, log the retry and compare pass rates of retried vs first-attempt trials (Inspect's distribution-shift warning); never re-run a trial that produced a pass/fail result.
- Unequal per-task N after exclusions is handled naturally by per-task pass rates and the hierarchical bootstrap (Q3); trial-level pairing cannot handle it.

### Gaps
- HAL (Holistic Agent Leaderboard, ICLR 2026) policy on timeouts/infra errors was not found in the retrieved excerpts.
- No source quantifies how infra-error exclusion biases a paired A-vs-B difference; the direction-of-bias argument above is inferred from Segato's "would have failed anyway" observation.
