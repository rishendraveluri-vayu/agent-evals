# LLM-as-judge methodology and human-label validation (state as of November 2026)

Scope: how LLM judges are designed, where they fail, how they are validated against human labels, and what the humans in the loop actually do. Written for a platform in which a rubric-based judge grades roughly 6 to 8 of 20+ agent tasks, must be blind to harness and model, and is validated against roughly 60 to 80 labels from one person with Cohen's kappa and per-config agreement reported.

Reading conventions: each finding carries its publication date where it matters, because this field moves fast. Numbers come from the primary paper or doc unless a bullet says "via search summary" or "secondary", in which case treat them as unverified. Several arXiv full texts were read through a summarising fetch; where that fetch flagged internal inconsistencies in the paper, the note says so.

## Key question 1: Judge design. Pointwise vs pairwise, rubric and criterion-level verdicts, reference-guided grading, quoted evidence, chain-of-thought, trajectories vs final answers

### Takeaway
The best-supported design for grading agent outputs against a rubric is pointwise, criterion-level binary verdicts (one isolated judge call per criterion or failure mode), with a reference or expected end state where one exists, a short rationale, and an explicit "Unknown / insufficient evidence" exit. Pairwise comparison agrees with humans slightly better on open-ended chat quality but imports position bias and is exacerbated by adversarial content; chain-of-thought before the verdict helps on reasoning-heavy grading but has negligible effect on tool-calling traces and creates an attack surface when the judge reads the agent's own reasoning.

### Cited Findings
- Pointwise vs pairwise (2023): MT-Bench found GPT-4 single-answer grading agreed well with both pairwise GPT-4 and humans; it "may sometimes perform slightly worse than pairwise comparison and give more tie votes, [but] it is a more scalable method". Pairwise grows quadratically in the number of systems; absolute scores "may fluctuate more if the judge model changes". — [Zheng et al., Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Pairwise is not uniformly more robust (2024): PRePair reports that evaluator biases "are exacerbated in pairwise evaluation", that pointwise "is less susceptible to such bias because each output is judged in isolation", and proposes integrating pointwise reasoning inside a pairwise frame to improve on the adversarial LLMBar benchmark while still beating pointwise on MT-Bench (no magnitudes on the abstract page). — [PRePair, arXiv 2406.12319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12319)
- Practitioner split (Aug 2024): Eugene Yan recommends direct (pointwise) scoring for objective criteria such as factuality or toxicity, because "a better option may still be a defect", and pairwise for subjective quality where it is "typically used, and more reliable"; pairwise gave little gain on factual consistency in the studies he reviewed. — [Eugene Yan, Evaluating the Effectiveness of LLM-Evaluators](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)
- OpenAI's platform guidance (fetched Nov 2026) lists pairwise, single-answer and reference-guided grading, recommends "pass/fail for more reliability" alongside pairwise, and says "Add reasoning and chain-of-thought as reasoning before scoring improves eval performance" and "Use the most capable model to grade if you can." — [OpenAI, Evaluation best practices](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices)
- Binary verdicts over Likert: Hamel Husain recommends binary pass/fail plus a written critique and says 1 to 5 scales and multi-metric dashboards "aren't actionable and often don't correlate with expert judgments". — [Hamel Husain, LLM-as-a-Judge guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/); the Husain and Shankar FAQ adds "Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling" and that Likert points are subjective, need larger samples and invite middle-value defaults. — [Husain and Shankar, AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Braintrust (Apr 2026): "Low-precision scales, such as 0 to 3 or pass/fail, usually produce more consistent scoring than 1-to-10 scales"; each score level should have a written definition and at least one example. — [Braintrust, Human-in-the-loop evals](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/human-in-the-loop-evals-for-llm-apps)
- Isolated per-dimension judges (Jan 2026): Anthropic recommends grading "each dimension with an isolated LLM-as-judge rather than using one to grade all dimensions", warns "Vague rubrics produce inconsistent judgments", and advises giving the grader "a way out", such as returning "Unknown" when information is insufficient, to reduce hallucinated verdicts. — [Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- One judge per failure mode: the Husain and Shankar FAQ says the most common mistake is asking one judge to catch "too many different kinds of errors at once", and to "build a separate judge for each type of failure" and "Give each judge only the parts of the trace it needs for its failure mode." — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Criterion-level binary rubrics validated at scale (May 2025): HealthBench grades 48,562 physician-written criteria independently as met / not met with point weights from -10 to +10; the GPT-4.1 grader reached macro-F1 0.709 against physician grades on 34 consensus criteria (over 60,896 meta-examples), beating the average physician on five of seven themes. — [HealthBench, arXiv 2505.08775](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)
- Hierarchical rubric judge for agent work (Apr 2025): PaperBench decomposes each replication into 8,316 leaf-node criteria; its SimpleJudge with o3-mini scored macro-F1 0.83 against human leaf grades (o1 0.84, GPT-4o 0.73, GPT-4o-mini 0.59, random 0.49) at about $66 per paper; the judge did worst on "Code Development" criteria (0.72) and best on "Result Match" (0.94). — [PaperBench, arXiv 2504.01848](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01848)
- Reference-guided grading (2023): on 10 math questions the GPT-4 judge's failure rate dropped from 14/20 (70%) with the default prompt to 3/20 (15%) with a reference answer; chain-of-thought alone only reached 6/20 (30%) because the judge "makes exactly the same mistake as the given answers". — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Reference helps, but can anchor (2026): AgentJudgeBench (3,808 tool-calling instances, six judges) found alignment degrades "1.5x faster without ground truth", yet supplying ground truth reduced alignment for GPT-5.4 (1.5 pp) and Gemini-2.5-Pro (3.9 pp), attributed to over-anchoring; structured rubrics improved alignment by up to 6.5 pp but not uniformly; chain-of-thought and judge temperature had "negligible effects". — [AgentJudgeBench, arXiv 2608.26623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26623)
- Reference as "sophisticated fuzzy matching" (2024): in Prometheus, removing the reference hurt judge performance the most; in PoLL, GPT-4 over-reasoned on reference checks until told not to overthink. — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)
- Chain-of-thought: CALM (ICLR 2025) measured a "CoT bias" where GLM-4 gained about 7 accuracy points with CoT but GPT-4-Turbo only about 0.7. — [Ye et al., Justice or Prejudice (CALM)](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736). For reward-model style grading, CoT plus majority voting had mixed effects on attack robustness (Qwen2.5-72B false-positive rate fell from 66.8% to 50.9% but Qwen2.5-7B rose from 12.6% to 40.4%). — [One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge, NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08794v3)
- Trajectory vs outcome (Jan 2026): Anthropic distinguishes the transcript (what the agent said and did) from the outcome ("the final state in the environment at the end of the trial") and says it is "often better to grade what the agent produced, not the path it took", so graders do not punish valid but unexpected approaches; model graders with rubrics can still assess "how the agent calls tools or interacts with the user". — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- Intermediate-step judging (Oct 2024): Agent-as-a-Judge on DevAI (55 tasks, 365 hierarchical requirements) aligned with human consensus 83.9% to 92.1% across three agent frameworks, vs 60.4% to 84.2% for plain LLM-as-a-Judge, at 2.29% of human cost and 2.36% of human time; the authors argue agentic systems should be evaluated on the full process, not just the final result. — [Zhuge et al., Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934)
- Two-phase agent evaluation (practitioner): first "Treat the agent as a black box and decide whether it met the user's goal", then once error analysis shows which workflows fail, add step-level checks; for tool calls, "Test the tool name, arguments, result, and resulting state as separate checks"; use a transition failure matrix (rows: last good state, columns: first failure) to locate where to look. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- More context can hurt the judge (2025): in AgentRewardBench the simplified judge did better with the screenshot alone (precision 64.5) than with screenshot plus accessibility tree (62.1); the authors conclude "more information distracts rather than assists the judge". A single-completion judge answering three questions beat prior multi-step judge designs on agreement with experts. — [AgentRewardBench, arXiv 2504.08942](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2)
- Judging the agent's narration is unsafe (Jan 2026): rewriting only the agent's chain-of-thought (actions and observations fixed) on 800 web-agent trajectories inflated judge false-positive rates substantially; "Progress Fabrication" raised GPT-4o and GLM-4.1V FPR by roughly 20 to 30 points (fetch rendered the figure as "2020-3030", read as 20-30), and removing CoTs from the judge input cut recall by up to 13 points for o4-mini. The authors call for "judging mechanisms that verify reasoning claims against observable evidence". — [Gaming the Judge, arXiv 2601.14691](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14691v2)
- Deterministic first: Anthropic's rule is "deterministic graders where possible, LLM graders where necessary or for additional flexibility"; the Husain and Shankar FAQ likewise says for CI "Favor assertions or other deterministic checks over LLM-as-judge evaluators." — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Mixing code and LLM assertions (2024): EvalGen participants preferred code for formatting, counting and include/exclude checks and LLM assertions for fuzzy criteria; LLM-based assertions were harder to trust. — [Shankar et al., Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)

### Inferences
- For 6 to 8 rubric-graded tasks compared across configs, pointwise criterion-level binary verdicts are the defensible default: they avoid position bias entirely, scale linearly in configs, give per-criterion TPR/TNR against the human labels, and match the designs that have been validated against humans at scale (HealthBench, PaperBench). Pairwise would only be worth it for a holistic "which transcript is better" question, and then needs the swap-and-tie protocol.
- Where a task has a checkable end state (file written, API call made, value returned), the judge should be given that state or a reference answer, and the rubric criterion should point at it. Reference-guided grading is the single largest accuracy lever in the literature (70% to 15% failure in MT-Bench's math case), but the AgentJudgeBench anchoring result argues for phrasing the reference as "expected outcome" rather than "the correct transcript".
- "Require quoted evidence" is best implemented as: the judge must cite the tool output or environment state (not the agent's reasoning text) that supports each criterion verdict, and may return "Unknown". The Gaming the Judge and AgentRewardBench results both show judges "readily agree with an agent's reasoning even when it is wrong", so evidence should be drawn from observations, not narration. Consider redacting or down-weighting agent reasoning in the judge input, accepting the recall cost.
- Chain-of-thought before the verdict is cheap and harmless for rubric grading of outcomes, but do not expect it to rescue a weak judge on tool-calling traces (negligible effect in AgentJudgeBench), and do not let its presence substitute for reading transcripts.

### Gaps
- No controlled head-to-head study was found that reports human agreement for pointwise and pairwise grading of the same agent tasks under the same rubric; the evidence is chat-quality (MT-Bench), adversarial instruction-following (PRePair) and practitioner opinion.
- I did not find a primary study isolating the effect of "require a verbatim quote as evidence" on judge accuracy; the closest evidence is indirect (Anthropic's "Unknown" exit, evidence-verification failures in REFLECT and Gaming the Judge).

## Key question 2: Known judge biases and failure modes

### Takeaway
Every documented bias (position, verbosity, self-preference, authority/formatting, sentiment, bandwagon, prompt sensitivity) persists in frontier judges at measurable magnitudes, judges are non-deterministic even at temperature 0, and they are trivially gameable by the graded system through fabricated progress claims, master-key tokens or null constant responses. Agent-transcript judging adds three specific failures: overestimating success (about 30% of "successes" are false in AgentRewardBench), a 37 to 50 point accuracy drop on long traces, and trusting the agent's own narration over observable state.

### Cited Findings
Position bias
- 2023, MT-Bench Table 2: with the default prompt, verdicts were position-consistent for only 65.0% of pairs with GPT-4 (30.0% biased to first, 5.0% to second), 46.2% with GPT-3.5, 23.8% with Claude-v1 (75.0% biased to first). Fix used: call the judge twice with swapped order and count a disagreement as a tie. — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- 2024-2025, 15 judges, over 150,000 evaluation instances on MTBench and DevBench: pairwise position consistency ranged 0.57 to 0.82 on MTBench and 0.23 to 0.89 on DevBench (Claude-3.5-Sonnet and GPT-4 0.82 on MTBench; Claude-3-Haiku 0.23 on DevBench). Position bias "is strongly affected by the quality gap between solutions" (near-equal answers flip most), is only weakly influenced by prompt length, and its direction varies by family (Claude models lean recency, +0.01 to +0.32; GPT models near zero or primacy). Capable judges show repetition stability above 0.85 (GPT-4 0.97). — [Shi et al., Systematic Study of Position Bias, arXiv 2406.07791](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.07791v9)
- 2025, CALM: position robustness rate ranged 0.566 (ChatGPT) to 0.832 (Claude-3.5), and with three or four candidates most models fell below 0.5. — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)
- 2026: GPT-4o-mini showed "significant first-position bias (72% A-majority, p = 0.024)". — [The Coin Flip Judge?, arXiv 2606.13685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685)

Verbosity and length bias
- 2023, MT-Bench Table 3 "repetitive list" attack: Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 failed on 91.3% of 23 padded answers, GPT-4 on 8.7%. — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- 2023: "GPT-4 prefers longer answers more than humans" (Saito et al.; magnitudes not on abstract page). — [Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling, arXiv 2310.10076](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10076)
- 2024: length-controlled AlpacaEval fits a GLM on length difference and predicts preference at zero length gap; this raised Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98 and "improves the robustness of the metric to manipulations in model verbosity". — [Length-Controlled AlpacaEval, arXiv 2404.04475](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475)
- 2025, CALM: verbosity robustness was high for frontier models on fact-based tasks (roughly 0.88 to 0.998), suggesting the crude bias has narrowed where answer correctness is objective. — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)
- OpenAI's current guidance still says "Control for response lengths as LLMs bias towards longer responses in general." — [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices)

Self-preference and model-family bias
- 2023: GPT-4 as judge gave itself a win rate about 10% higher than humans did; Claude-v1 about 25% higher; GPT-3.5 did not; the authors said data was too limited to be sure. — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- 2024: GPT-4 distinguished its own summaries from two other LLMs and humans with 73.5% accuracy out of the box (pairwise self-recognition 0.672 on XSUM, 0.747 on CNN/DM); pairwise self-preference 0.705 (XSUM) and 0.912 (CNN/DM) for GPT-4; fine-tuning for self-recognition produced a "strong linear correlation" with self-preference, and the authors suggest "authorship obfuscation could become standard practice". — [Panickssery, Bowman, Feng, LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076v1)
- 2024: GPT-4 shows significant self-preference, attributed to perplexity: judges prefer text more familiar to them (via search summary). — [Wataoka et al., Self-Preference Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge, arXiv 2410.21819](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.21819)
- 2025, CALM self-enhancement error rate: Qwen2 16.1%, ChatGPT 8.91%, Claude-3.5 7.48%, GPT-4-Turbo 1.16%, GLM-4 1.18%. "Compassion-fade" (judging differently when model names are shown vs anonymised) robustness was 0.835 to 0.877. — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)
- 2026: a paper on "self- and other-labels" reports bidirectional bias from authorship labels (title only, via search). — [arXiv 2608.18091](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18091)
- Unverified: a secondary write-up of a later Judge Arena study claims judges favoured their own answers about 70% more often than human voters and agreed with each other 79.4% but with humans only 56.9%; I could not find Atla's primary publication of this. — [remio.ai (secondary)](https://www.remio.ai/post/arena-llm-judge-bias-models-favor-their-own-answers-70-more-than-humans)

Authority, formatting, sentiment, bandwagon, distraction
- 2025, CALM: fake book-style citations cut ChatGPT's robustness to 0.628 (URL citations were less effective, Claude-3.5 at 0.884); bandwagon ("60% of people prefer A") cut Claude-3.5 to 0.563; adding negative emotion (especially fear) to the better answer dropped robustness to about 0.25 to 0.27 for GPT-4-Turbo and 0.24 to 0.26 for Claude-3.5, the sharpest drop measured; identity markers dropped ChatGPT to 0.646 on "Female". Bias effects are larger on alignment data where quality gaps are small. — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)
- Judges accept "Yes" and "Sure" as correct answers (dummy-answer vulnerability) and sometimes fail even on verbatim gold answers; under-specified answers were 37.3% of judge errors on TriviaQA, with GPT-4 Turbo recalling only 33.9% of them. — [Thakur et al., Judging the Judges: Alignment and Vulnerabilities, arXiv 2406.12624](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624)

Sensitivity to prompt wording
- 2024-2025: top judges (GPT-4 Turbo, Llama-3 70B/3.1 70B) stay aligned with a 45-token prompt and "slightly improve with more detailed prompts" (up to 301 tokens), while weaker judges "lose alignment with increased instructions"; smaller judges are also sensitive to reference order. — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624)
- The HealthBench team tuned both the grading prompt and the wording of individual criteria, and notes that because GPT-4.1 was used during that tuning it may be advantaged among graders tested. — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)
- Unverified: a blog claims judge behaviour swings "up to 40%" across prompt templates; no primary source found. — [memx.app (secondary)](https://memx.app/blog/llm-as-a-judge-biases-and-fixes/)

Non-determinism and test-retest of the judge
- Oct 2025, Rating Roulette: Krippendorff's alpha between three identical runs of the same judge was 0.33 (Llama-3.1-70B), 0.63 (DeepSeek-R1-Distill), 0.79 (Qwen3-32B) on binary SummaC, and 0.27 / 0.51 / 0.56 on pairwise MT-Bench; Qwen3 gave the same pairwise verdict in all three runs for only 61.3% of items. Disabling sampling reduced agreement with humans for every model (e.g. Qwen3 79.2 vs 80.6 with majority vote), so the authors recommend aggregating multiple runs rather than forcing determinism, and measuring human intra-rater reliability as the ceiling. — [Haldar and Hockenmaier, Rating Roulette, arXiv 2510.27106](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27106)
- Apr 2026: pairwise preferences flipped on average 13.6% of the time across 50 repeats; a majority vote needs about 11 trials to recover the 50-trial verdict with 95% probability (15 for high-variance items); deterministic decoding "reduces but does not eliminate inconsistency". — [The Coin Flip Judge?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13685) (A search summary of the same paper gave GPT-4o-mini's flip rate as 13.3% at default vs 2.8% at temperature 0; not confirmed on the abstract page.)
- Jun 2026: on 7 deliberately borderline items, 4 of 7 were non-reproducible at provider-default temperature (per-item disagreement up to about 50% over 20 runs), 2 of 7 at temperature 0, and 1 of 7 even with forced greedy decoding (top_k=1) on Claude Sonnet 4.6; recommendations are to pin temperature and seed, log the resolved model version and endpoint, run multiple epochs and report variance, and treat grader disagreement as a health metric flagging items for rubric revision or human review. — [Necessary but Not Sufficient, arXiv 2606.26185](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.26185)
- Feb 2026: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Claude-Haiku-4.5 and Claude-Sonnet-4.5 show "substantial variability" in repeated rubric scoring, with completeness the least stable criterion; lower temperature helped GPT and Gemini but had limited or inconsistent effect on Anthropic models. — [Same Input, Different Scores, arXiv 2603.04417](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04417)
- Aggregate scores can be stable even when item verdicts are not: HealthBench's overall score had SD about 0.002 over 16 runs (o3 mean 0.5990, SD 0.0016), including generation noise. — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)
- CALM defines a Consistency Rate (share of repeated unperturbed judgments that agree) as a separate metric from bias robustness. — [CALM](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02736)

Reward hacking of judges by the graded system
- 2024: a "null model" that "always outputs a constant response" irrelevant to the instruction reached an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto and 9.55 on MT-Bench. Related work cited: Raina et al. 2024 (short universal adversarial phrases appended to responses inflate judge scores) and Shi et al. 2024 (optimisation-based prompt injection biasing a judge toward a chosen response). — [Cheating Automatic LLM Benchmarks, arXiv 2410.07137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137)
- NeurIPS 2025: single "master key" strings ("Thought process:", "Solution", "Let's solve this problem step by step.", or just ":") produced false-positive rewards from general-purpose judges: overall average FPR 80.6% for LLaMA3-70B, 66.8% for Qwen2.5-72B, 14.6% for GPT-4o, 12.4% for Claude-4, 6.0% for GPT-o1 (worst case 37.3% to 95.1%). Larger judges were not more robust (Qwen2.5-72B 66.8% vs Qwen2.5-7B 12.6%). Training a reward model with truncated outputs as adversarial negatives cut average FPR to 0.1% (Master-RM-7B). Removing the question from the judge prompt dropped Qwen2.5-72B's GSM8K FPR from 87.6% to 0.0%. — [One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.08794v3)
- 2026: BITE, a bandit-guided black-box method, learns per-judge style manipulations that raise scores (via search summary). — [Turning Bias into Bugs, alphaxiv 2605.26156](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2605.26156.md)
- Agent-specific hacking (Jan 2026): content-based CoT fabrication ("Progress Fabrication", "Environment Blaming") was consistently stronger than style-only manipulation (roughly 5 to 10 point increases); a manipulation-aware prompt cut the FPR increase for o4-mini and Claude-Sonnet-4 from 7.5% to 3% and for GPT-4o from 27% to 18%, but barely helped Pixtral-12B (36% to 32%); majority voting over 4 to 32 samples cut GPT-4o's Progress-Fabrication delta only from about 93% to 84%; "absolute false positive rates remain high". — [Gaming the Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14691v2)
- Anthropic: "Make your graders resistant to bypasses or hacks." — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)

Failures specific to agent transcripts (long contexts, tool outputs)
- Apr 2025, AgentRewardBench (1,302 web-agent trajectories, 6 expert annotators): no LLM judge exceeded 70% precision on success, so about 30% of trajectories judged successful were not; best F1 was 75.9 (GPT-4o simplified judge, precision 69.8, recall 83.1). Documented failure modes: accepting the agent's claim it reached the right product when it opened the wrong page; accepting a claimed filter that was never applied; counting a "found the cereal" report as a completed purchase; not flagging an agent that wrongly declared a feasible task infeasible. Rule-based graders had the opposite problem (precision 83.8, recall 55.9), understating GPT-4o's success by 16.7 points on WebArena. — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2)
- 2024 precursor: Pan et al. found the best LLM judge reached 80.6% accuracy against WebArena's rule-based evaluator (cited in AgentRewardBench). — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2)
- NeurIPS 2026, TraceJudgeBench (unifies ten judge-validation datasets): established judges (TRAIL, AEGIS, a prompted LLM) showed false-error rates of 34% to 78% on clean traces (flagging errors where none exist), the prompted baseline sat at 67% to 78% across three model families, and "every judge degrades a further 37 to 50 points on long traces", an effect hidden by averaging. — [TraceJudgeBench](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/173089)
- EMNLP 2026, AgentJudgeBench: judge alignment "degrades monotonically with task difficulty"; on hard queries without ground truth all six judges (20B to frontier) converged to a 77 to 82% band, attributed to task difficulty rather than model scale. — [AgentJudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26623)
- May 2026, REFLECT (evidence-based research agents): "even the best-performing models achieve overall accuracies below 55%" at detecting injected process- and outcome-level failures, with judges "especially poor" on evidence verification. — [REFLECT, arXiv 2605.19196](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19196)
- 2026: transcript-based judges "expose a prompt-injection surface through tool outputs" that memoryless, call-level judges do not (via search summary). — [ScopeJudge, arXiv 2607.07774](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.07774)
- Agent-as-a-Judge human baseline: human experts themselves "missed critical information, such as environment feedback showing small coding errors or bugs" in long agent traces. — [Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934)
- Leniency: judges that do not follow the criteria default to "correct" (P+ of 0.87 to 0.99 for several open models), i.e. the failure direction is usually false pass. — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624)

### Inferences
- The platform's judge will be biased toward passing. Every agent-transcript study finds over-estimation of success and leniency, so the human-label validation must report TPR and TNR separately and the held-out set must contain enough genuine failures to estimate TNR at all.
- Blinding the judge to the model and harness name is necessary (CALM compassion-fade, authority bias) but not sufficient: GPT-4 recognised its own prose at 73.5% without any labels. If one of the graded configs uses the same model family as the judge, self-preference is a live confound and should be checked explicitly in the per-config agreement numbers. Using a judge from a family not under test, or two judges from different families, is the cleanest mitigation.
- Harness blindness in practice means normalising the transcript: strip system prompts, tool-schema boilerplate, harness-specific formatting and model self-identification before the judge sees it, and feed the judge observable state (tool outputs, final artefacts) rather than the agent's reasoning text wherever the rubric allows.
- Judge non-determinism must be treated as a measured quantity, not assumed away by temperature 0. A test-retest run of the judge on the human-labelled subset (same inputs, k repeats, report Krippendorff's alpha or flip rate per criterion) is cheap and directly comparable to the literature above.
- Long transcripts are where judges fail hardest. If a task's transcripts are long, prefer grading the end state or a deterministic extract over grading the full trace, or truncate to the evidence the criterion needs.

### Gaps
- No primary source was found quantifying how much judge accuracy differs between agent configurations (e.g. harness A vs harness B) when the judge is blind; the self-preference and compassion-fade results are about model identity, not scaffolds.
- The TraceJudgeBench numbers come from a conference listing without the paper; the "100%" figure for the decomposed ensemble without abstention is ambiguous in the listing and is not reported above as a finding.
- Magnitudes for verbosity bias on agent transcripts specifically (as opposed to chat answers) were not found.

## Key question 3: Validation against humans. Label counts, agreement statistics, thresholds, test-retest, per-configuration validation

### Takeaway
Reported validation sets range from 16 grades (EvalGen) through 100 to 200 per failure mode (Husain and Shankar) to thousands (MT-Bench 3K votes, HealthBench 60K meta-examples); chance-corrected statistics (Cohen's kappa, Scott's pi, Krippendorff's alpha) or TPR/TNR are preferred over raw agreement, which is "rarely discriminable" across judges; the only widely accepted threshold is "about as good as humans agree with each other", with the classic 0.61 to 0.80 "substantial" kappa band and Krippendorff's 0.667 / 0.800 cut-offs used as rough anchors and explicitly called arbitrary. Almost nobody validates per graded configuration; the two closest practices are per-theme reporting (HealthBench) and per-agent-framework reporting (Agent-as-a-Judge).

### Cited Findings
How many human labels
- 2023: MT-Bench used about 3,000 expert votes from 58 graduate-student labelers (each judged at least 20 questions) plus 3,000 Chatbot Arena votes sampled from about 30,000. — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- 2024: EvalGen's offline evaluation used 16 graded outputs per pipeline (vs 80 to 100 for the SPADE baseline); study participants graded 5 to 10 outputs before defining criteria and one said "you should enforce that we all look at at least 20 examples first." — [Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)
- Hamel Husain: start with about 30 examples and keep adding until no new failure modes appear; for validation aim for about 100 per failure mode with enough Pass and Fail; "Below 60 examples, the confidence intervals are often too wide to support a useful conclusion." — [LLM-as-a-Judge guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)
- Husain and Shankar FAQ: label "100 to 200 examples for each failure mode"; split 10 to 20% train (few-shot only), 40 to 45% dev, remainder a single-use test set; aim for 30 to 50 Pass and 30 to 50 Fail in both dev and test; for debugging disagreement at least 50 passing and 50 failing. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Arize (Jul 2026): no universal minimum; worst-case proportion estimate at 95% confidence needs about 100 examples for roughly plus or minus 10 points and about 400 for plus or minus 5; use 30 to 50 representative examples for early rubric iteration; at about 100 examples "treat results as directional"; count examples per class because rare classes are the binding constraint (at 5% failures a 200-example set has about ten failures). — [Arize, Measuring human-LLM judge alignment](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- LangSmith docs: start with "at least 20 examples", balanced between 0 and 1, and add more later to avoid overfitting. — [LangSmith, Improve judge evaluator feedback](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback)
- Anthropic: for early-stage agents "20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start"; large effect sizes mean "small sample sizes suffice". — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- Benchmarks: AgentRewardBench 3,906 binary annotations over 1,302 trajectories; HealthBench over 60,896 meta-examples (34 criteria, 1,072 to 3,370 each); PaperBench human-graded partial replications of five papers; AMA-Bench validated its judge on 300 human-labelled instances at 92.67% accuracy (via search summary). — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2); [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1); [PaperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01848); [AMA-Bench, arXiv 2602.22769](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.22769)

Which statistics are reported
- Percent agreement, non-tie: MT-Bench reports GPT-4 vs human 85% (859 and 864 votes, turns 1 and 2) and 87% on Chatbot Arena (1,944 votes), vs human-human 81% and 82%. — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Why raw agreement misleads: Thakur et al. found percent agreement "rarely discriminable" across judges, "even judges that have more than 90% may still differ more than 10 points in their assigned score", and exact-match showed a 26-point gap from humans in percent agreement but a 64-point gap in Scott's pi; Llama-3 8B had percent agreement "well above 80%" but Scott's pi of 59. Three human annotators on 1,200 questions had Scott's pi 96.2 and 98.52% agreement; top judges reached pi "in the high 80s", 8 points behind humans. The paper dropped Cohen's kappa for Scott's pi citing "well-documented theoretical issues". — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12624) (Eugene Yan's summary of an earlier version gives Cohen's kappa 0.84 for GPT-4, 0.79 for Llama-3-70B vs 0.97 human-human. — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/))
- Hamel Husain: treat the judge as a binary classifier and report TPR and TNR separately; a judge that always says Pass gets 95% agreement when only 5% fail; his Honeycomb example reached ">90% agreement" in three iterations but he warns raw agreement misleads with imbalanced classes. — [LLM-as-a-Judge guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)
- Husain and Shankar: if multiple annotators, "Cohen's Kappa, which accounts for agreement beyond chance"; for judge validation track TPR and TNR "as you make changes"; once TPR/TNR are known the judge's estimated failure rate can be corrected. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Arize's statistic selection: Cohen's kappa for two raters with categorical labels (weighted kappa for ordinal); Fleiss' kappa for three or more raters labelling everything; Krippendorff's alpha for uneven coverage or missing labels; precision, recall, F1 against an adjudicated reference with the positive class fixed in advance; report raw agreement alongside kappa because severe imbalance can produce low kappa with high raw agreement (the kappa prevalence paradox); bootstrap 95% CIs over whole traces, paired bootstrap when comparing judges. — [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- Macro-F1 of met / not-met as the grader statistic: HealthBench (0.709), PaperBench (0.83), LiveMedBench and ClinConsensus reuse the same protocol (via search summary). — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1); [PaperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01848)
- Krippendorff's alpha across repeated judge runs as the intra-rater statistic. — [Rating Roulette](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27106)
- Eugene Yan: Cohen's kappa for binary/categorical, Kendall's tau or Spearman's rho for Likert (but these do not correct for chance); UMbrela showed kappa 0.3 to 0.5 while tau/rho were 0.8 to 0.9, "showing how conservative kappa is". — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)
- LangSmith's "alignment score" is simply the percentage of examples where the evaluator matches the human label. — [LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback)
- EvalGen defines coverage (fraction of human-bad outputs the assertion fails), false failure rate (fraction of human-good outputs it fails) and alignment as their harmonic mean. — [Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)

Thresholds people use
- Landis and Koch (1977) kappa bands: 0.21-0.40 fair, 0.41-0.60 moderate, 0.61-0.80 substantial, 0.81-1 almost perfect; Fleiss: over 0.75 excellent, 0.40-0.75 fair to good, below 0.40 poor. Wikipedia notes Landis and Koch "supplied no evidence to support it, basing it instead on personal opinion", that the guidelines "may be more harmful than helpful", and that "no one value of kappa can be regarded as universally acceptable". — [Wikipedia, Cohen's kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen%27s_kappa)
- Krippendorff's alpha: rely on data with alpha >= 0.800, draw only tentative conclusions for 0.667 <= alpha < 0.800, discard below 0.667, and set the bar higher when mistaken conclusions are costly. — [Wikipedia, Krippendorff's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha)
- Arize explicitly warns: "Avoid relying on universal thresholds such as '0.6 is good' or '0.8 is acceptable.'" Sufficiency depends on task ambiguity, decision risk and how the judge is used; human agreement "is a baseline, not a hard ceiling". — [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- The de facto threshold is human-human agreement: MT-Bench (85% vs 81%), HealthBench (grader 0.709 vs physician-physician macro-F1 0.569 to 0.730 by theme; both model-physician and physician-physician raw agreement 55 to 75%), Anthropic ("closely calibrated with human experts"), OpenAI ("consistently agrees with human annotations"). — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685); [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1); [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices)
- Human ceilings are often low: AgentRewardBench's second annotator agreed 89.3% on success; Agent-as-a-Judge's three experts disagreed pairwise 10% to 30%, with individual alignment to consensus 76.2% to 92.6% (majority vote 94.0 to 95.1%); HealthBench physicians disagreed at the case level in about 22.5% of cases per a 2026 re-analysis, with 81.8% of disagreement variance residing at the case level and borderline-quality completions driving most of it (inverted-U, AUC 0.689). — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2); [Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934); [Decomposing Physician Disagreement in HealthBench, arXiv 2602.22758](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22758)
- Secondary thresholds in circulation (not primary research): an MLflow article suggests kappa >= 0.61 trustworthy and < 0.40 needs rubric revision; a blog suggests below 0.6 "downstream numbers are dominated by judge noise"; a practitioner guide suggests two reviewers on 100 to 200 stratified items and trusting the judge only where it matches adjudicated labels about as well as humans match each other. — [MLflow (via search)](https://mlflow.org/articles/tags/judge-language-model-accuracy/); [boundev.ai (secondary)](https://www.boundev.ai/blog/inter-rater-agreement-llm-eval-kappa-alpha)
- Human labels are themselves noisy: "'Gold' and 'ground truth' human-mediated labels have error", and "using standard metrics in the presence of poor labels can mask both label and model quality"; GPT-assisted labelling "would worsen reliabilities for humans influenced by these models". — [Hardy, All that Glitters, arXiv 2411.15634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15634)

Test-retest of the judge
- See Rating Roulette (alpha 0.27 to 0.79 across three runs), Coin Flip Judge (13.6% flips, 11 trials for 95% recovery), Necessary but Not Sufficient (1 to 2 of 7 borderline items non-reproducible even with greedy decoding), Shi et al. (repetition stability above 0.85 for capable judges), CALM's Consistency Rate, HealthBench (aggregate SD 0.002 over 16 runs). Sources as cited in question 2.
- Rating Roulette recommends also collecting intra-rater data for the human annotators "since this would set an upper bound for what LLM judges can reasonably achieve". — [Rating Roulette](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27106)

Per-configuration or per-model validation
- HealthBench reports grader macro-F1 per theme (0.572 to 0.706) and grader percentile among physicians per theme (37.5 to 88.2), i.e. stratified by task family, not by graded model. — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1)
- Agent-as-a-Judge reports alignment per graded agent framework: 88.5% / 83.9% / 90.4% (MetaGPT / GPT-Pilot / OpenHands) for the agent judge vs 84.2% / 65.3% / 60.4% for the LLM judge, so judge accuracy varied by up to 24 points depending on which system was graded. — [Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934)
- AgentRewardBench reports judge precision per benchmark and per agent and found "no single LLM excels across all benchmarks"; rule-based grading reversed the ranking of Qwen2.5-VL and GPT-4o on WebArena relative to experts. — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2)
- AgentJudgeBench notes that rubric gains "don't generalize uniformly across judge-generator pairs". — [AgentJudgeBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26623)
- Thakur et al. tested 13 judges on nine exam-taker models (base and instruction-tuned) and report that even weak judges and a lexical metric gave a reasonable ranking of the nine models despite poor absolute alignment. — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)
- AMA-Bench cross-validated its Qwen3-32B judge against four other judge families (agreement within 8.1 points) in addition to 300 human labels (via search summary). — [AMA-Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.22769)

### Inferences
- A 60 to 80 label budget sits right at Hamel's "below 60 the CIs are too wide" floor and below Arize's 100-for-plus-or-minus-10-points guide. It is enough to estimate overall per-criterion agreement with wide intervals, and not enough to estimate TNR unless failures are deliberately over-sampled. Plan: stratify the labelled set so that roughly 30 to 50% of items are judge-predicted failures (and say so), report kappa and TPR/TNR with bootstrap CIs, and keep a small natural-distribution subset separately.
- Per-config agreement on 60 to 80 labels across several configs means roughly 15 to 25 labels per config. Kappa on 20 items is a point estimate with an interval wider than the Landis-Koch bands themselves, so per-config numbers should be reported as descriptive (raw agreement and counts of disagreements, with CIs), used to flag a config whose disagreement pattern is one-directional (e.g. the judge is lenient only for configs sharing its model family), not as a gate.
- Because configs will have different true pass rates, kappa will differ across configs even if the judge is equally good, through the prevalence paradox. Report raw agreement and the 2x2 confusion counts next to kappa for each config, as Arize recommends.
- The most citable reference point for "good enough" is "within the human-human range for comparable tasks": roughly 0.81 to 0.89 raw agreement on binary outcome labels for agent traces (MT-Bench, AgentRewardBench) and far lower for borderline-quality rubric items (HealthBench). For a single-labeller design there is no human-human number, so a self-consistency relabel (question 4) is the substitute.

### Gaps
- No primary paper or vendor doc was found that recommends or demonstrates validating a judge per graded configuration and reporting per-config kappa; the per-system reporting in Agent-as-a-Judge and AgentRewardBench is the closest precedent. This appears to be a genuine methodological gap rather than something I failed to find.
- No source gives a sample-size rule for kappa at a target CI width in the LLM-judge setting; Arize's numbers are for proportions, not kappa.
- The Atla Judge Arena has no published results paper that I could find; only the launch post (GPT-4 Turbo narrowly first, Llama 3.1 70B second, 405B third, no vote counts). — [Judge Arena launch post, Nov 2024](https://huggingface.co/blog/arena-atla). JudgeBench accuracies (GPT-4o 56.57%, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.29%) come from a search summary of a review, not the paper.

## Key question 4: Human labelling protocols. Blind labelling, stratified sampling, single rater, rater self-consistency, adjudication, rubric iteration with separate tuning and held-out sets, tools

### Takeaway
Current practice converges on: one accountable domain expert ("principal domain expert" / "benevolent dictator") who labels binary pass/fail with a written critique, in a lightweight custom tool, on a sample stratified by feature, scenario and failure mode; criteria are expected to drift during labelling (so tuning labels are kept apart from the final test set and the rubric is frozen before the test set is scored once); with multiple raters, disagreements are resolved by independent labelling, an alignment session, rubric update and expert tie-break. Rater self-consistency checks are recommended in the literature but rarely practised, and blind labelling (hiding judge scores and model identity from the rater) is more implied than documented.

### Cited Findings
- Single accountable rater: pick "one (maybe two)" principal domain expert whose judgment defines success; do not substitute a developer or convenient proxy. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/). The FAQ: for most small and medium companies "one domain expert as a 'benevolent dictator'"; "Outsourcing error analysis is usually a big mistake", except for mechanical tasks with a fixed rubric. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Single raters are unreliable in absolute terms: Agent-as-a-Judge found pairwise disagreement between three experts of about 10% to 30%, "the inherent unreliability of a single human evaluation", with one evaluator erring 23.77% on GPT-Pilot; a debate round to consensus took 28.5 extra hours on top of 58 hours of labelling. — [Agent-as-a-Judge](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10934)
- Criteria drift: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria"; participants added new criteria after seeing new bad outputs and reinterpreted existing ones (a "proper noun" rule loosened to "most" entities after seeing varied outputs); the authors conclude criteria depend on outputs observed, which "challenges methods that assume fixed criteria defined beforehand". Grading was binary thumbs-up / thumbs-down; a thumbs-up is assumed to pass all criteria. — [Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)
- Open coding before rubric: open code 30 to 50 traces yourself before any LLM-assisted grouping. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Sampling: structure data across features, scenarios and personas and ensure coverage; combine real and synthetic inputs (LLMs generate inputs only, never responses); after the first two iterations focus on errors while keeping some random sampling; compute failure rates by dimension on unseen data only. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/). Braintrust lists random, priority (low automated scores, high latency, user reports), stratified and edge-case sampling, and suggests 50 to 100 traces per week as a starting review volume. — [Braintrust](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/human-in-the-loop-evals-for-llm-apps)
- Enriching failures: if you over-sample failures, disclose it, and keep a natural-distribution test set separate from the failure-focused set. — [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- Critique format: critiques must be detailed enough for a few-shot prompt and understandable to a new employee; terse critiques are a common mistake; the expert writes their own critiques in a shared spreadsheet of inputs, outputs, critiques and outcomes while the engineer refines the judge prompt. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)
- Tuning vs held-out split: 10 to 20% train (few-shot only), 40 to 45% dev (inspect errors, revise prompt; dev examples never go in the prompt), 40 to 45% test "kept hidden until the prompt is frozen, then run once"; if test is much worse than dev, suspect overfitting. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/); [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/). LangSmith: validate on new labelled examples after prompt iteration to avoid overfitting. — [LangSmith](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback)
- Adjudication with multiple raters: label independently, hold an alignment session, update the rubric, relabel affected examples, expert makes the final call. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/). AgentRewardBench: six expert annotators, disagreements "resolved through discussion", 89.3% second-annotator agreement on success. — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2). HealthBench: physicians graded whether each criterion was met; agreement computed as each physician's macro-F1 against the others. — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1). Arize: two annotators leave every disagreement tied, so plan a third or an adjudication step. — [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- Rater self-consistency: Rating Roulette says NLP annotation and LLM-evaluation studies "often leave out" intra-rater reliability even though essay grading and clinical diagnostics routinely report it, and recommends collecting it for human annotators. — [Rating Roulette](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27106)
- Read the transcripts: "You won't know if your graders are working well unless you read the transcripts and grades from many trials"; "We do not take eval scores at face value until someone digs into the details of the eval and reads some transcripts"; failures should be fair, i.e. "it's clear what the agent got wrong and why". — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- Re-review cadence: repeat expert review at regular intervals and after material changes such as a model update; review "at least 100+ fresh traces each review cycle", typically every 2 to 4 weeks; phase out always-passing evals, expensive LLM-judge evals first. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/); [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/). Anthropic: once robust "it's sufficient to use human review only occasionally". — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- Tools: build a lightweight custom annotation interface or use a notebook; "teams with custom annotation tools iterate ~10x faster"; vendor tools fit when coordinating many distributed annotators with enterprise access controls, but "many teams find the configuration overhead and limitations aren't worth it". — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/). AgentRewardBench used a custom Gradio interface. — [AgentRewardBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08942v2). LangSmith uses annotation queues feeding a reference dataset and Align Evals (launched 29 Jul 2025; boolean evaluators only at launch per a LangChain forum reply, via search). — [LangSmith docs](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback); [Align Evals launch](https://blog.langchain.com/introducing-align-evals). Braintrust keeps human review scores and automated scores on the same traces in one project. — [Braintrust](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/human-in-the-loop-evals-for-llm-apps)
- Judge should not see what the rater sees differently: the FAQ says to hand-validate the labels used to test judges because "LLMs can make mistakes that lead to unreliable benchmarks", and to run ablations on which parts of the trace each judge needs. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Human-in-the-loop contamination: GPT-assisted labelling "would worsen reliabilities for humans influenced by these models". — [All that Glitters](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15634)

### Inferences
- For a single-rater design the three cheap reliability safeguards are: (1) label blind to judge output, config and model name (present the normalised transcript the judge will see, nothing more); (2) relabel a random 15 to 20% of items after a gap of days with items shuffled, and report intra-rater agreement (kappa or raw) as the human ceiling the judge is compared against; (3) write a one-line critique per label so disagreements with the judge can be classified later as rubric ambiguity, rater error or judge error. None of these is standard in the sources, but each follows directly from Rating Roulette, Arize and Hamel's recommendations.
- Criteria drift means the rubric will change while the first labels are made. The split that protects the held-out set is: label a tuning batch first, let the rubric drift and freeze it, then label the held-out batch under the frozen rubric, score it once. Labels made under the earlier rubric version should be re-checked or excluded, with the change recorded.
- Stratified sampling for this platform should stratify on task, config and judge-predicted verdict so that every config has both predicted passes and predicted failures in the labelled set; otherwise per-config TNR is unestimable.

### Gaps
- No source documents a blind-labelling protocol specifically for validating an LLM judge (hiding model/config from the rater); it is implied by "reading transcripts" advice and by the compassion-fade result but not spelled out.
- No published protocol or expected value was found for single-annotator intra-rater reliability on agent-transcript labels.

## Key question 5: What to do when a judge is below threshold. Drop the task, fall back to human labels, or report with caveats

### Takeaway
The literature's ordered response is: inspect the disagreements by hand and classify them; split the judge into one-per-failure-mode and tighten context or rubric wording; swap in a stronger or different-family judge or add reference/end-state information; if still weak, keep the judge for ranking or triage only and fall back to deterministic checks or human labels for that task; always report the judge's measured TPR/TNR and either correct the estimates or caveat them. Nobody recommends silently dropping a task; the closest is Anthropic's observation that near-zero scores usually mean a broken task or grader, which is a reason to fix or exclude the task openly.

### Cited Findings
- First inspect, do not auto-tune: "inspect disagreements manually before trying automated prompt tuning"; the most common cause is one judge asked to catch too many error types, so "build a separate judge for each type of failure"; also check for missing context, vague instructions and unclear success definitions. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Adjust prompts by hand; the author "has had little success with prompt optimizers"; avoid fine-tuning judges by default; use the most powerful model the budget allows. — [Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)
- Try a different model if alignment is poor, but switching providers is costly so not the default; start with the strongest model, then test cheaper ones and measure the accuracy loss; "using the same model is usually fine" because the judge's task is narrower. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Correct rather than discard: once TPR and TNR are known, "you can adjust its estimated failure rate"; track CIs and investigate when the lower bound crosses the threshold. — [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Judges are "not reliable enough to systematically replace human judgments"; use them for a few hundred samples in development, fine-tune a classifier for production guardrails; non-expert annotators agreed more with judges than experts did, so reported alignment may be inflated. — [Eugene Yan](https://eugeneyan.com/writing/llm-evaluators/)
- Weak judges can still rank: smaller judges and even a lexical metric gave a reasonable ranking of nine exam-taker models despite poor absolute alignment; the authors urge caution in more complex setups. — [Thakur et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)
- Fix the task or grader before concluding anything: "a 0% pass rate across many trials" is "most often a signal of a broken task, not an incapable agent"; Opus 4.5 went from 42% to 95% on CORE-Bench after fixing "rigid grading that penalized '96.12' when expecting '96.124991...'", ambiguous specs and stochastic tasks; METR's time-horizon tasks penalised models for following instructions. — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- Deterministic fallback: "deterministic graders where possible"; for CI "Favor assertions or other deterministic checks over LLM-as-judge evaluators". — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents); [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Levers with measured effect sizes: reference answer (70% to 15% failure, MT-Bench math); structured rubric (+6.5 pp, AgentJudgeBench); manipulation-aware prompt (FPR delta 7.5% to 3% for strong judges, Gaming the Judge); majority vote over repeats (recovers the stable verdict with about 11 trials, Coin Flip Judge; improves human agreement, Rating Roulette); removing the question from the judge prompt (87.6% to 0.0% FPR in the master-key setting, One Token); removing agent CoT from judge input (robustness up, recall down up to 13 points). Sources as cited above.
- Hybrid human-LLM: "monitoring, robust parsing, and hybrid human-LLM evaluation strategies" are needed for dependable production judging. — [Same Input, Different Scores](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04417). Braintrust: code checks for structure, LLM judges for subjective dimensions, human review for the highest-stakes or most ambiguous cases. — [Braintrust encyclopedia (via search)](https://www.braintrust.dev/encyclopedia/llm-as-a-judge)
- Items with high judge disagreement across repeats "are candidates for rubric revision or human review". — [Necessary but Not Sufficient](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.26185)
- Judge-flagged human labels at scale: EvalGen used selectivity and grades to estimate confidence that an ungraded output is poor, pointing humans at likely failures. — [Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)
- Never drop failures silently and record plan changes: this is project policy rather than external literature (see CLAUDE.md in the repo), but it is consistent with Arize's "if you enrich with failures, disclose it" and Anthropic's "read the transcripts".

### Inferences
- A sensible decision rule for this platform, given 60 to 80 labels: (a) if a criterion's judge agreement is within the bootstrap CI of the rater's own self-consistency, use the judge and report kappa, TPR/TNR and CIs; (b) if below, first split or reword the criterion and re-run on the dev portion only; (c) if still below after one or two iterations, demote that criterion to "judge-assisted triage": the judge flags, the human labels every flagged item plus a random sample of unflagged items for that task, and the reported metric is the human label; (d) if the task cannot be human-labelled at the required volume, report the judge score with an explicit caveat and the per-config agreement table, not as a headline result; (e) excluding a task entirely is a last resort and should be recorded as a deviation with the agreement numbers that motivated it.
- Per-config disagreement that is one-directional (judge lenient on one config) should trigger step (c) for that config, not a global rubric change, because changing the rubric after seeing per-config results is exactly the analysis-plan drift the project rules forbid without recording it.

### Gaps
- No source gives an explicit decision tree for "drop vs human fallback vs caveat"; the above is synthesised from guidance written for other settings.
- No source quantifies how much a "judge flags, human confirms" protocol reduces labelling cost on agent transcripts relative to blind labelling; EvalGen's confidence scoring is the only related mechanism found.

## Key question 6: Guidance from Anthropic, OpenAI, LangChain, Braintrust, Arize, Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar, MT-Bench and Judge Arena

### Takeaway
Vendor guidance is consistent and conservative: isolate rubric dimensions, binary or low-precision scales with written level definitions, strongest available model with reasoning before the verdict, calibrate against human labels and keep calibrating, read transcripts, prefer deterministic checks. The academic literature (MT-Bench 2023 through the 2026 agent-trace benchmarks) supplies the magnitudes the vendors omit and is more pessimistic about agent-trace judging than the vendor docs are.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic (Jan 2026): grader taxonomy of rubric scoring, natural-language assertions, pairwise comparison, reference-based evaluation and multi-judge consensus; "LLM-as-judge graders should be closely calibrated with human experts" and research rubrics "should be frequently calibrated against expert human judgment"; isolated per-dimension judges; "Unknown" exit; partial credit for multi-component tasks; multiple trials because outputs vary (pass@k vs pass^k; 75% per-trial success gives pass^3 of about 42%); isolate trials from a clean environment (Claude once read git history from previous trials); "Ambiguity in task specifications becomes noise in metrics." No confidence-interval or sample-size guidance. — [Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)
- OpenAI (platform docs, fetched Nov 2026): pairwise, single-answer and reference-guided graders; "pass/fail for more reliability"; scale up "once the LLM judge reaches a point where it's faster, cheaper, and consistently agrees with human annotations"; ignoring human calibration is an anti-pattern; "Use the most capable model to grade if you can" (the doc names gpt-6-astra as a starting point); "Add reasoning and chain-of-thought as reasoning before scoring improves eval performance"; "Control for response lengths"; "Ensure eval rubrics are clear and detailed"; show examples at each score level and add a pass/fail threshold to numeric scores. — [OpenAI, Evaluation best practices](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/evaluation-best-practices)
- OpenAI's own validated graders: HealthBench (GPT-4.1 grader, macro-F1 0.709 vs physicians, chosen because it "achieves physician-level agreement without the costs and latency of reasoning models") and PaperBench (o3-mini SimpleJudge, F1 0.83, "not as accurate as an expert human", non-deterministic). — [HealthBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775v1); [PaperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01848)
- LangChain / LangSmith (Jul 2025 onward): Align Evals; humans label via annotation queues into a reference dataset; alignment score = percent match; start with at least 20 balanced examples; iterate the prompt against misaligned examples; enable reasoning to see why the judge scored as it did; validate on fresh labels; "isn't an exact science", no target threshold. — [LangSmith docs](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback); [Align Evals launch](https://blog.langchain.com/introducing-align-evals)
- Braintrust (Apr 2026): "Human review scores can serve as ground truth for calibrating LLM-as-a-judge scorers"; compare automated and human scores on the same traces and refine the scorer prompt; low-precision scales with definitions and examples; 50 to 100 traces per week review as a starting point; no agreement statistic or threshold named; blind review not mentioned. — [Braintrust](https://www.braintrust.dev/articles/human-in-the-loop-evals-for-llm-apps). An unverified secondary claim puts Braintrust's recalibration drift window at 60 to 90 days. — [Grepture (secondary)](https://grepture.com/blog/llm-as-a-judge-bias)
- Arize (Jul 2026): statistic-selection guide (kappa / weighted kappa / Fleiss / Krippendorff / per-class F1), no universal thresholds, about 100 labels for plus or minus 10 points, count per class, disclose failure enrichment, bootstrap CIs over whole traces, illustrative human-human 69 to 75% vs LLM-human 68 to 71% as "operating near the human range". — [Arize](https://arize.com/blog/measuring-human-llm-judge-alignment/)
- Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar: principal domain expert; binary plus critique; 100 to 200 labels per failure mode; train/dev/test split; TPR/TNR over raw agreement; one judge per failure mode with minimal context; custom annotation tool; 100+ fresh traces every 2 to 4 weeks; black-box first then step-level for agents; transition failure matrix. — [LLM-as-a-Judge guide](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/); [AI Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)
- Shankar et al., EvalGen (2024): criteria drift; coverage / FFR / alignment; nine practitioners; 16 grades per pipeline; code assertions trusted more than LLM assertions; per-criterion FFR thresholds suggested. — [Who Validates the Validators](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12272)
- MT-Bench (2023): the baseline numbers for the whole field (85% vs 81%, position/verbosity/self-enhancement magnitudes, swap-and-tie, reference-guided). — [MT-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685)
- Judge Arena (Atla, Nov 2024): users see two judges' scores and rationales, vote for the one they agree with, names revealed after the vote; Elo leaderboard; preliminary ranking GPT-4 Turbo narrowly first, Llama 3.1 70B second, 405B third, with small open models "remarkably" competitive; no vote counts published. — [Judge Arena launch post](https://huggingface.co/blog/arena-atla). JudgeBench (2024): GPT-4o about 56.57% and Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.29% accuracy on hard pairs, only modestly above chance (via search summary of a review). — [JudgeBench review (secondary)](https://liner.com/review/judgebench-a-benchmark-for-evaluating-llmbased-judges)
- Surveys (Nov and Dec 2024) define the bias taxonomy (task-agnostic vs judgment-specific; position, verbosity, self-enhancement) but do not add magnitudes. — [A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge, arXiv 2411.15594](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.15594); [LLMs-as-Judges survey, arXiv 2412.05579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05579)

### Inferences
- The vendor docs agree with each other on design (isolated binary criteria, strongest model, human calibration) and disagree only on how much to formalise the statistics; Arize is the only vendor source that gives a defensible statistics protocol, and Hamel/Shankar the only practitioner source with a concrete split-and-threshold workflow. Citing those two plus MT-Bench and HealthBench covers the "why" for every design choice the platform makes.
- The 2026 agent-trace literature (TraceJudgeBench, AgentJudgeBench, REFLECT, Gaming the Judge) is markedly more negative than the vendor docs about judging full transcripts. A platform that grades mostly end states and short extracts, and says so, is on firmer ground than one claiming to grade trajectories.

- Atla's later Judge Arena analysis, if it exists, was not found; the self-preference figures circulating about it are unverified.
